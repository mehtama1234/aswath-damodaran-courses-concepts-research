Life-cycle valuation

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Life-cycle valuation

Meaning. A company should be valued differently depending on whether it is young, scaling, mature, declining, private, distressed, or being acquired.

Why it matters. The same template cannot carry every firm; the stage of the business changes the plausible story and the right inputs.

How Damodaran develops it. The course explicitly pauses to value companies across the life cycle after building the core DCF machinery.

Common mistakes

  • Using mature-company margins for startups.
  • Using startup growth stories for declining firms.

Worked example

Compare three companies at different life stages. Amazon in 2005 (young, high growth): revenue $8.5B, terminal growth 8%, reinvestment rate 120% of earnings (burning cash to scale), ROIC 8% (low, reinvesting to build scale), cost of equity 10%. The arithmetic reveals the paradox: value-of-growth = (ROIC - cost of equity) × reinvestment rate / (cost of equity - growth) = (0.08 - 0.10) × 1.20 / (0.10 - 0.08) = -0.02 × 1.20 / 0.02 = -1.2× base earnings. At 8% ROIC below a 10% cost of capital, Amazon's aggressive 120% reinvestment rate was *destroying* ~120% of base earnings value, not creating it. Yet Amazon's valuation remained high because investors believed ROIC would improve as scale matured—a *belief* about future ROIC, not current fundamentals. By 2020, Amazon's ROIC had risen to 15%, finally exceeding cost of capital, so incremental reinvestment began creating value.

Microsoft in 2015 (mature, stable): revenue $93B, terminal growth 3%, reinvestment rate 25% of earnings, ROIC 30% (established moat), cost of equity 8%. Value from growth = reinvestment rate × ROIC × growth / (cost of equity - growth) = 0.25 × 0.30 × 0.03 / (0.08 - 0.03) = 0.00225 / 0.05 = 0.045 per dollar of base earnings—disciplined, because ROIC (30%) far exceeds cost of capital (8%), so even 25% reinvestment rate creates stable value. The multiple is modest because mature companies have limited growth.

Kodak in 2008 (declining): revenue still $12B, but terminal growth -1%, shrinking margins (25% to 18%), negative reinvestment (harvesting capital), cost of equity 9%. Present value of those shrinking cash flows = far less than book value of assets because the decline was structural.

The unifying principle: growth creates value only when reinvestment returns exceed cost of equity AND the growth rate justifies the reinvestment. Amazon at 8% ROIC couldn't justify growth at any rate when cost was 10%; it was a bet that ROIC would improve. Mature companies like Microsoft create steady value when ROIC stays above cost of capital. Declining companies destroy value no matter how much capital you redeploy.

Where the approach breaks

Life-cycle valuation fails when you lock in the wrong stage. Cisco in 2000 was treated as perpetual high-growth; it was actually entering maturity, and valuations that assumed 25% growth forever and 50% reinvestment for 20 years crashed 80% when reality hit. Reverse failure: treating General Motors as perpetually mature when bankruptcy risk was rising meant missing the option-like value embedded in restructuring. The deepest failure is *not* revising the stage as evidence arrives. Tesla was reasonably young-company in 2015 (high reinvestment, thin margins, explosive growth); by 2022 it was scaling into mature territory (ROIC rising, growth moderating, free cash flow positive). If you re-valued it in 2022 with 2015 assumptions (30% terminal growth, 150% reinvestment rate, 5% ROIC), you'd wildly overprice it. Stage has to be tied to *evidence*: ROIC trending, margin trajectory, capex intensity, and whether the company is still fighting for market share.
