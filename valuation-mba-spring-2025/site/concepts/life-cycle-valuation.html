Life-cycle valuation

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Life-cycle valuation

Meaning. A company should be valued differently depending on whether it is young, scaling, mature, declining, private, distressed, or being acquired.

Why it matters. The same template cannot carry every firm; the stage of the business changes the plausible story and the right inputs.

How Damodaran develops it. The course explicitly pauses to value companies across the life cycle after building the core DCF machinery.

Common mistakes

  • Using mature-company margins for startups.
  • Using startup growth stories for declining firms.

Worked example

Compare three companies at different life stages. Amazon in 2005 (young, high growth): revenue $8.5B, terminal growth 8%, reinvestment rate 120% of earnings (burning cash to scale), ROIC 8% (low, reinvesting to build scale), cost of equity 10%. Present value of growth = 8% × (1.10 - 1) / (0.10 - 0.08) × base earnings = massive multiple. Microsoft in 2015 (mature, stable): revenue $93B, terminal growth 3%, reinvestment rate 25% of earnings, ROIC 30% (established moat), cost of equity 8%. Value = base cash flow / (0.08 - 0.03) + growth value. The growth contributes 25% × 30% × 3% / (8% - 3%) = 0.45 per dollar of base earnings—disciplined, not explosive. Kodak in 2008 (declining): revenue still $12B, but terminal growth -1%, shrinking margins (25% to 18%), negative reinvestment (harvesting capital), cost of equity 9%. Present value of those shrinking cash flows = far less than book value of assets because the decline was structural.

The mechanism: growth creates value only when reinvestment returns exceed cost of equity. Amazon reinvested at 8% cost (low ROIC), but growth of 30%+ paid for it because growth was 4× the cost. By 2020, Amazon's ROIC had risen to 15%, so same growth created more value per dollar reinvested. At terminal maturity, ROIC should converge to cost of capital; if it stays above, you're either missing the cyclical trough or misunderstanding the business.

Where the approach breaks

Life-cycle valuation fails when you lock in the wrong stage. Cisco in 2000 was treated as perpetual high-growth; it was actually entering maturity, and valuations that assumed 25% growth forever and 50% reinvestment for 20 years crashed 80% when reality hit. Reverse failure: treating General Motors as perpetually mature when bankruptcy risk was rising meant missing the option-like value embedded in restructuring. The deepest failure is *not* revising the stage as evidence arrives. Tesla was reasonably young-company in 2015 (high reinvestment, thin margins, explosive growth); by 2022 it was scaling into mature territory (ROIC rising, growth moderating, free cash flow positive). If you re-valued it in 2022 with 2015 assumptions (30% terminal growth, 150% reinvestment rate, 5% ROIC), you'd wildly overprice it. Stage has to be tied to *evidence*: ROIC trending, margin trajectory, capex intensity, and whether the company is still fighting for market share.
