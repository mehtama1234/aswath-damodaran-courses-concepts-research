Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Liquidity and ownership

Meaning. Liquidity, diversification, and control rights affect value when the owner cannot freely trade or diversify the asset.

Why it matters. This shifts valuation from a clean security-market problem to a problem of who owns the asset and under what constraints.

How Damodaran develops it. Private-company and control sessions make ownership frictions explicit rather than treating them as footnotes.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the same discount for every private firm.
  • Ignoring whether the buyer is diversified or has control.

Worked example

A family-owned manufacturing business is valued at $100M using DCF (intrinsic value = public-market equivalent). But the owner is 65, has no heir, and owns 100% of net worth. The owner cannot diversify—if the business fails, personal net worth collapses. A public-market investor can hold 1% of the business in a diversified portfolio; the owner holds 100%. Damodaran applies a liquidity discount: a non-diversified, illiquid stake commands a discount of 25-35% to reflect that the owner cannot sell quickly and bears concentrated risk. Adjusted value = $100M × (1 - 0.30) = $70M.

Contrast: the same business bought by a PE firm that will eventually exit via IPO or sale commands closer to $95-100M because the PE investor is diversified, has an exit timeline (7-10 years), and has tools to professionalize operations. The delta of $25-30M is not mispricing; it reflects the owner's constraints versus the buyer's optionality.

Where the approach breaks

Liquidity discounts collapse when applied mechanically. A 30% discount assumes the owner will never find a buyer and faces a fire sale. But a strategic buyer or PE acquirer may pay full value—the discount is only real if you condition on no informed buyer showing up. Historical case: private companies with a clear exit path (e.g., VCs with Series-C funding rounds) command no liquidity discount; discounts apply when the owner is truly trapped. Red flag: if you apply the same discount to every private firm regardless of sector (tech startups ≠ commodity manufacturers), you are not thinking about liquidity, you are applying superstition.
