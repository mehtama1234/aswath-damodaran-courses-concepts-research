Concept

Private company valuation

Meaning. Private-company valuation changes when the marginal owner, diversification, control, illiquidity, and information quality differ from public markets.

Why it matters. It forces the analyst to stop pretending the public-market template always applies.

How Damodaran develops it. The private-business session brings owner-specific risk, liquidity discounts, and control into the valuation problem.

Common mistakes

  • Using public-company betas without adjusting for owner risk.
  • Treating illiquidity as a generic arbitrary discount.

Worked example

An owner of a $50M EBITDA private manufacturing firm seeks to value it for sale. Using a public-company multiple—say, 8× EBITDA = $400M—omits three private-company realities: (1) the owner is entirely undiversified and deserves a 200 bps illiquidity discount; (2) the firm has no public market for partial exit, so the discount rate for illiquid equity rises from 10% to 12%; and (3) the buyer likely needs to pay a control premium to replace the departing owner's knowledge and relationships. Applying the 12% discount rate to normalized EBITDA of $50M growing at 3% with 40% reinvestment yields a terminal value of (50M × 0.40 × 0.03 / (0.12 - 0.03)) = $66.7M versus the firm's perpetual cash generation at 10% (which would give $166.7M). The 200 bps illiquidity premium alone reduces value by roughly 15-20%.

A second example: a private equity firm buys this business at $200M, installs management, and sells it five years later at 9.5× EBITDA (achieved through professional operations) to a strategic buyer. The buyer now has liquidity (can go public or be acquired) and diversification. The same $50M EBITDA command a 9.5× multiple = $475M, not because the business changed, but because liquidity and ownership structure changed.

Where the approach breaks

Private valuation breaks when analysts apply public-market betas and tax-neutral assumptions to undiversified owners. A private-firm beta from unlevered peers is not a private owner's beta; the owner bears company-specific risk with no portfolio hedge. Adding leverage to a private firm pushes its equity beta and cost of equity up sharply—a 50% debt ratio often implies an equity cost 3-4 points higher than a public peer. Illiquidity discounts are also easy to game: claiming a 30% haircut for illiquidity on an otherwise-stable business is indefensible. The hard case is when the private business is changing (new ownership, management, market access). Valuing a private firm about to go public must distinguish the value as private today from the value it will command as public tomorrow; analysts often conflate them, pricing as if liquidity is already present.
