Concept

DCF big picture

Meaning. A DCF values an asset by forecasting expected cash flows and discounting them at rates that reflect timing, risk, and currency.

Why it matters. It is the central architecture for intrinsic value and the reference point for understanding why shortcuts can mislead.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran builds DCF from first principles before adding the hard inputs: riskfree rates, premiums, cash flows, growth, and terminal value.

Common mistakes

  • Treating DCF as a template.
  • Mixing cash-flow definitions and discount-rate definitions.

Worked example

A software company generates $100M in free cash flow today and is expected to grow at 8% annually for 5 years, then 2.5% in perpetuity. The cost of equity is 10% (reflecting the company's tech-sector risk). Discount the year-1 cash flow: $100M × 1.08 = $108M, discounted at 10% = $108M / 1.10 = $98.2M. Year-5 cash flow = $100M × (1.08^5) = $147.0M, discounted back 5 years = $147.0M / (1.10^5) = $91.3M. Terminal value at year 5 is calculated as Year-6 cash flow ($147.0M × 1.025) divided by (cost of equity minus perpetual growth): ($150.7M) / (0.10 - 0.025) = $2,009M, discounted to today = $2,009M / (1.10^5) = $1,247M. Summing all discounted cash flows gives intrinsic value. The final number depends entirely on three inputs: the cash-flow forecast, the discount rate, and the terminal-growth assumption.

If you change the perpetual growth rate from 2.5% to 3%, the terminal value jumps to $150.7M / 0.07 = $2,153M—a 7% increase in total firm value from a single percentage-point shift. This illustrates why DCF is powerful but also why it is sensitive: small changes in long-term growth or cost of capital create outsized value swings. The method is honest because it forces you to name every assumption; the limitation is that precision beyond two decimal places is false comfort.

Where the approach breaks

The DCF method breaks down when cash flows are unpredictable or perpetual growth assumptions are unrealistic. During the 2008 financial crisis, investment banks used DCFs to value mortgage-backed securities by assuming house prices would not decline nationwide—an assumption that looked reasonable in hindsight only after a $2 trillion asset class collapsed. Similarly, very high-growth companies (like Amazon in the 1990s) are treacherous because a 1% error in terminal growth assumes the company will capture an unrealistic share of world GDP. The approach also fails if the business model is structural: a utility company earning a regulated 5% return has little room for optimism, while a biotech firm with one FDA-pending drug has cash flows that swing between zero and billions depending on binary outcomes. When cash-flow uncertainty exceeds ±30% or terminal value exceeds 70% of total value, treat the DCF as a stress-test framework, not a point estimate.
