Market PE ratios

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Market PE ratios

Meaning. Market PE ratios price entire markets by relating index prices to earnings and macro expectations.

Why it matters. The concept shows that pricing logic applies at the market level too, where rates, growth, risk premiums, and mood collide.

How Damodaran develops it. The tariff and market PE session applies pricing language to market-wide multiples and macro narratives.

Common mistakes

  • Reading market PE without considering rates and growth.
  • Treating macro narratives as if they bypass valuation math.

Worked example

The S&P 500 trades at a 22x trailing price-to-earnings ratio on 2024 earnings of $230 per share, implying a market value of roughly $5,060 per share. That ratio looks like a simple number but is actually compressing three hidden assumptions: that the market expects 5% earnings growth (embedded in trend expectations), that equity risk premium is 5.5% (cost of equity minus risk-free rate), and that interest rates will stabilize near current levels (2.5% risk-free). If you buy the market at 22x PE without examining these assumptions, you're betting all three are correct. Now, if interest rates spike to 4% (a 150 basis-point increase, which happened in 2022–2023), the risk-free rate rises and the equity risk premium must tighten to maintain the market's willingness to hold stocks—the PE ratio might compress to 18x. At that PE, the same $230 earnings stream is worth only $4,140 per share, a 18% loss. Conversely, if growth accelerates to 7% and the market resets forward, the PE expands to 25x, and value rises to $5,750. The ratio is not stable; it is a temporary snapshot of market consensus on risk and growth.

Where the approach breaks

Market PE ratios break down during regime shifts or when comparing across economic cycles. The S&P 500 traded at 34x PE in 1929 at the peak of the bubble, then at 7x PE in 1982 at the trough of stagflation and high rates—the same market, 53 years apart, trading at a 5x multiple swing because interest rates and growth expectations had inverted. Using a simple PE multiple to value a cyclical company (like banking or oil) is treacherous: a bank with $2 earnings and a 12x PE looks cheap at $24, but if the economy enters recession and earnings fall to $1, the PE expands to 24x on the same price because risk rises and growth becomes negative. The failure threshold is when comparing a high-growth company (like Tesla at 60x PE) to a mature company (like Ford at 5x PE); naively concluding Ford is cheaper misses that the market is pricing in opposite growth, profitability, and competitive outcomes. PE ratios are most reliable when comparing peers in the same industry during stable rate environments; they fail completely as valuation shortcuts when rates, competition, or industry structure are in transition.
