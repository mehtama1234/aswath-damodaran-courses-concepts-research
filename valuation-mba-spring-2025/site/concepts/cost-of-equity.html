Cost of equity

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Cost of equity

Meaning. The cost of equity is the return required by equity investors for bearing the risk of residual cash flows.

Why it matters. It is the equity discount rate and a central input in both firm and equity valuation.

How Damodaran develops it. The course builds it from riskfree rates, equity risk premiums, country risk, and relative risk measures.

Common mistakes

  • Treating cost of equity as what managers hope to earn.
  • Using the same cost of equity for businesses with different risk.

Worked example

Take a mature pharmaceutical firm trading at $100 per share. The risk-free rate is 4% (10-year Treasury). Equity risk premium is 5% (what stocks return above the Treasury). Beta is 0.9 (drugs are slightly less volatile than the market). Cost of equity = 4% + (0.9 × 5%) = 8.5%. Now value its 5-year free cash flow of $5B per year and terminal value. Discount Year 5 terminal value ($75B, growing at 2% perpetually) at 8.5%: that terminal value is $75B / (8.5% - 2%) = $1.154 trillion in Year 0. The numerator here is 8.5%, the discount rate driven by cost of equity. Now change one input: if that firm moves into a new high-risk market (cancer biotech), its beta rises to 1.4. New cost of equity = 4% + (1.4 × 5%) = 11%. The same $75B terminal value now discounts to $75B / (11% - 2%) = $833B. The terminal value shrunk $321B—28% of total value—because the cash flows got riskier, not because the business changed. This is why using one corporate cost of equity for different-risk projects destroys discipline: a mature drug at 8.5% versus early biotech at 11% should get different hurdle rates in capital allocation.

Where the approach breaks

Cost of equity breaks when inputs are stale, borrowed from incomparable firms, or divorced from reality. First failure: a company in a low-risk industry (utilities, 0.6 beta) applies the same 10% cost of equity to a venture into emerging markets (which should carry 1.8 beta, 12.5% cost of equity). They overfund risky projects because the hurdle rate is too low. Second failure: using a 5% equity risk premium in 2022 when valuations were at historical highs and forward-looking premiums implied 3-4% (Damodaran's implied premium work shows this). Too low a premium inflates all equity values. Third failure: emerging-market firms ignore country risk. A Brazilian company might have beta 1.2 (vs global 1.0) but also faces default risk; Damodaran's approach adds 2-3% country risk spread on top. Ignoring this makes Brazil-specific projects look twice as cheap. The boundary: if your cost of equity has not been updated in over a year, if it comes from a peer group that is neither similar nor recent, or if it ignores currency and country risk, your terminal value is fantasy arithmetic.
