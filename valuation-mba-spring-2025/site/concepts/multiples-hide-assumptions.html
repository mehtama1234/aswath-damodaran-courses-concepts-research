Concept

Multiples hide assumptions

Meaning. A multiple compresses assumptions about growth, risk, margins, capital intensity, and cash-flow quality into one market ratio.

Why it matters. Multiples feel simple because assumptions are hidden; the analyst's job is to make those assumptions visible again.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran repeatedly decomposes PE, EV/EBITDA, revenue multiples, and other ratios into their fundamental drivers.

Common mistakes

  • Using a multiple without defining it consistently.
  • Ignoring growth and risk differences across comparable firms.

Worked example

You see Company A trading at 18x forward P/E and Company B at 12x forward P/E. Without unpacking assumptions, you might think B is cheap. Now decompose the multiples using the fundamental P/E formula: P/E = (Payout Ratio × (1 + Growth Rate)) / (Cost of Equity - Growth Rate). For Company A: assume 40% payout, 8% growth, 11% cost of equity. P/E = (0.40 × 1.08) / (0.11 - 0.08) = 0.432 / 0.03 = 14.4x. But the market shows 18x. The hidden assumption: either growth is 10% (not 8%), or cost of equity is lower (9.5%), or payout is higher (50%). For Company B: assume 60% payout, 4% growth, 10% cost of equity. P/E = (0.60 × 1.04) / (0.10 - 0.04) = 0.624 / 0.06 = 10.4x. Market shows 12x. Hidden assumptions: either growth is 6%, or cost of equity is 8.5%, or payout is 70%. Now the question: are those hidden assumptions realistic? If A's growth assumption of 10% is justified (market share gains, new products), the 18x is fair. If B's hidden 6% growth assumes market saturation, the 12x is fair—and B is not cheap, it is mature. The multiples stopped hiding when you made the assumptions explicit and testable.

Where the approach breaks

Multiples fail when assumptions diverge from fundamentals or when the comparable set contains hidden mismatches. First failure: during the dot-com bubble, internet companies traded at 50x+ revenue multiples. The hidden assumption: zero customer acquisition cost at scale and negative cash-conversion cycles. When those assumptions broke (because they were false), valuations collapsed 80-90% in months. Second failure: comparing a pure-play cloud-software company (70% gross margins, 30% growth) to a diversified software conglomerate (45% gross margins, 8% growth) using the same EV/Sales multiple. The conglomerate's assets are bundled wrong into one multiple. Third failure: using EV/EBITDA for capital-intensive businesses without accounting for reinvestment. Airlines at 6x EV/EBITDA look cheap until you realize they need $1.5B per year in capex just to stay flat (that capex is hidden, not in EBITDA). The boundary: the minute peers contain companies with materially different growth, risk, or reinvestment needs, one multiple applies to all of them. That multiple is hiding structural differences, not compressing comparable economics.
