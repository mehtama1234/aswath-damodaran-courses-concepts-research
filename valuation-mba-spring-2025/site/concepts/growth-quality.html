Growth quality

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Growth quality

Meaning. Growth is high quality only when it earns more than it costs, can be funded realistically, and does not destroy margins or returns.

Why it matters. The course refuses to treat growth as automatically good; growth can destroy value when reinvestment economics are weak.

How Damodaran develops it. Growth, cash-flow, and terminal-value sessions repeatedly connect growth to returns on capital and reinvestment intensity.

Common mistakes

  • Rewarding revenue growth without checking returns.
  • Assuming scale automatically improves economics.

Worked example

A fast-growing software firm forecasts 25% annual revenue growth over 5 years. To sustain that growth, it requires 60% of revenues reinvested in R&D, sales, and infrastructure. Its operating margin is 15%. That means the firm earns 0.15 × Revenue and must reinvest 0.60 × Revenue, leaving only 3% of revenues as cash flow for the business. If the discount rate is 9% and terminal growth is 3%, the terminal value per dollar of revenue converges to (0.03 / (0.09 - 0.03)) = 0.50, meaning the valuation leans entirely on switching to a mature-business model at year 6. By contrast, a competitor growing at 12% annually needs only 40% reinvestment, leaving 9% of revenues as cash flow, yielding a terminal multiple of (0.09 / (0.09 - 0.03)) = 1.50 per revenue dollar—3 times the first firm's value despite lower headline growth.

The return spread matters more than growth rate. If a firm grows 20% annually but earns only 8% returns on invested capital (ROIC), while the cost of capital is 9%, that firm is destroying value with each increment of growth. Only when ROIC exceeds the discount rate—when growth quality turns positive—does expansion add value.

Where the approach breaks

Growth quality fails when a firm projects high growth without credible reinvestment economics. A telecom claiming 20% subscriber growth while maintaining 30% operating margins should trigger a flag: how is it funding expansion? If through massive capex not yet reflected in margins, those margins will compress. If through acquisition debt, financial leverage will rise and cost of equity will climb, offsetting nominal growth benefits. The clearest historical failure: internet firms in 1999-2000 projected hypergrowth (50%+ annually) with promises of profitability 'later,' discounting at rates that assumed they'd eventually earn 15% returns. They rarely did. The boundary is crossed when reinvestment intensity required to hit growth forecasts would leave the firm with returns below its cost of capital—at which point growth becomes destructive, not creative.
