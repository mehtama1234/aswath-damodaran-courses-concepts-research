Concept

Pricing 101

Meaning. Pricing estimates what an asset should trade for by looking at similar assets and the market's current pricing language.

Why it matters. It is powerful because markets often transact on relative pricing, but it should not be confused with intrinsic value.

How Damodaran develops it. The pricing block starts by separating pricing from valuation before moving into multiples and peer groups.

Common mistakes

  • Calling pricing valuation.
  • Choosing comparables to justify a desired price.

Worked example

Three tech peers trade at: Company A 22x P/E, Company B 18x P/E, Company C 15x P/E. All earn $100M net income. At the median peer multiple of 18x, a comparable company should be worth $1.8B. But pricing is not intrinsic value—it is what the market paid for the peer group. The multiple reflects the market's collective bet that these businesses will grow at certain rates, face certain risks, and reinvest capital at certain returns. Company A trades at 22x not because it is worth more in absolute terms, but because investors are pricing in faster growth (35% vs. 20% for C) or lower risk (beta 0.9 vs. 1.3). When the analyst applies 18x to a new company, they are saying 'this target has the same growth and risk profile as the median peer.' If that claim is false, the multiple is misleading.

Where the approach breaks

Pricing breaks when the analyst forgets what the multiple hides. Two dangers: First, the chosen peer group matters enormously. Selecting peers to justify a desired valuation is manipulation—the result looks like analysis but is driven by peer selection bias. Real case: a declining legacy retailer was valued at 12x P/E using Amazon and Costco as peers; that was absurd given the true peer set was Sears and Macy's. Second, multiples can be driven by temporary market mood, not fundamentals. In 2021, unprofitable software firms traded at 10-15x revenues; by 2023, profitable ones traded at 1-2x. The same businesses, radically different multiples. Pricing analysis was true at both points—it just showed what the market was willing to pay, not what the businesses were worth. An analyst who valued only on multiples would have missed the repricing entirely.
