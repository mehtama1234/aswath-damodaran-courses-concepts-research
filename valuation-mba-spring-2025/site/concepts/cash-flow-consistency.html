Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Cash-flow consistency

Meaning. Cash flows have to match the claim being valued, the currency chosen, the inflation assumption, and the discount rate.

Why it matters. Most wrong valuations are not wrong because the math fails; they are wrong because the model combines incompatible assumptions.

How Damodaran develops it. The early DCF sessions make consistency the hidden law that governs currency, nominal versus real inputs, taxes, debt, and equity claims.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting equity cash flows at a cost of capital.
  • Mixing real cash flows with nominal discount rates.

Worked example

A software company earns EBIT of $50M on $200M revenue, with a 25% tax rate and 8% net working capital increase on new revenue. Its debt costs 5% pre-tax (3.75% after-tax), equity 12%, and it has a 60% debt / 40% equity capital structure. Computing free cash flow to firm: Operating income after tax = $50M × (1 - 0.25) = $37.5M. Add back capex of $8M (maintenance). Subtract working capital increase of $16M. FCFF = $37.5M - $8M + $16M = $45.5M. The cost of capital must match: WACC = 0.60 × 3.75% + 0.40 × 12% = 7.65%. If you instead discounted equity cash flows (owner's residual after debt) at the 7.65% WACC, you would understate equity holders' required return and misvalue the equity slice. This is the core consistency: equity flows meet equity rate; firm flows meet firm rate.

Where the approach breaks

Consistency breaks when firms operate in multiple currencies or when macro conditions force a sharp inflation shift. Example: a US company with Indian subsidiaries earning rupees. If you forecast the subsidiary's rupee cash flows and discount at a dollar cost of capital, you violate consistency because the inflation embedded in the rupee rate doesn't match the dollar rate. The rupee might carry 8% inflation; the dollar 2%. A 6% nominal rupee cost of capital is not comparable to a 5% dollar cost of capital without adjusting one to the other's inflation base. In the 2022–2023 inflation shock, many models broke because analysts kept 2020's 2% inflation in their terminal growth but used 2023's 5% riskfree rates—mixing old real assumptions with new nominal inputs. The answer's sensitivity is extreme: a terminal-value model can swing 20–30% from small currency or inflation mismatches.
