Equity risk premiums

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Equity risk premiums

Meaning. The equity risk premium is the market price of bearing equity risk over a riskfree asset.

Why it matters. It is one of the biggest levers in valuation and should reflect current market pricing, not just inherited historical averages.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran develops historical, implied, and country-adjusted premiums as competing ways to estimate the market price of risk.

Common mistakes

  • Using a stale historical premium without checking current market levels.
  • Ignoring country risk in global businesses.

Worked example

In January 2025, US Treasury 10-year yield stood at 4.0%. Historical equity risk premium (1926-2024) averaged 5.2%, but implied premium from S&P 500 valuations at P/E 20 and expected earnings growth 5% yields only 3.8%. An analyst using the implied 3.8% ERP would require equity return of 4.0% + 1.1 beta × 3.8% = 8.18%, versus 9.72% using historical 5.2%. That 150bp difference cuts a $100M equity value by roughly 12-15%.

For an emerging market like Mexico, add a country risk premium: sovereign spread of 120bp × (equity volatility 28% / bond volatility 12%) = 280bp. So cost of equity = 4.0% + 1.2 × 3.8% + 2.8% = 11.46%. If valuations assume historical 5.2%, cost of equity jumps to 13.24%, cutting terminal value sharply.

Where the approach breaks

Historical ERP becomes unreliable when market risk appetite shifts regime. The 5.2% premium assumed normal leverage and volatility; in 2008, realized ERP compressed to near zero as equity risk repriced upward. Using historical 5.2% in a 2000-2001 tech bust would have overstated equity appeal by 300+ bps. Second failure: country risk premiums assume spread-equity volatility correlation holds; in currency crises (Mexico 1994, Brazil 1999), equity beta to default risk spikes, rendering the scaled spread formula obsolete until market stabilizes.
