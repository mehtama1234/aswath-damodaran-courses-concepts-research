Concept

Acquisition valuation

Meaning. Acquisition valuation asks what a target is worth to a buyer, including standalone value, synergy, control changes, and deal process constraints.

Why it matters. It separates value creation from value transfer: paying more only makes sense if the buyer can create or capture incremental value.

How Damodaran develops it. Late sessions connect distressed equity, acquisition pricing, synergy, and value enhancement before the final control discussion.

Common mistakes

  • Paying for synergy without specifying who creates it.
  • Confusing strategic desire with incremental value.

Worked example

Company A (buyer) wants to acquire Company B (target). B's standalone value (as if independent) is $100M via DCF. However, A can realize $20M in synergies: $12M from cost reductions (overlapping sales teams, admin) and $8M from revenue synergies (cross-selling). A is willing to bid up to $110M (standalone $100M + synergies $20M). The seller has competing bids and expects $105M from another buyer. In the negotiation, A offers $112M. The economics: A pays $112M, realizes $20M in synergies, and creates value of $120M - $112M = $8M for its shareholders. But if A overpays at $125M, it destroys $5M of shareholder value immediately ($120M synergies - $125M paid). Control premiums matter: if A must pay a 40% control premium to unseat the incumbent board, the "fair" price to a strategic buyer is much higher than the stock's standalone trading price.

Also critical: who realizes the synergy? If A can cut B's costs by $12M annually (present value $60M), but B's seller gets a $65M bid from a PE firm that will also cut those costs, then A is bidding against the PE buyer for the same $60M prize. A's true winning bid should assume no excess synergy—or accept that the PE buyer will capture the synergies and a financial return is sufficient.

Where the approach breaks

Acquisition valuation breaks when synergies are imagined rather than specified. From 1998–2001, dealmakers promised cost synergies from mega-mergers (DaimlerChrysler, AOL-Time Warner) that never materialized. DaimlerChrysler paid $40B for Chrysler (acquiring $10B in standalone value at the time) and promised $3B in annual synergies; a decade later, they were separating. When synergy is vague ("we will improve their processes"), buyers overpay. Second, when the buyer's cost of capital is higher than it assumes: if A pays $120M for a $100M asset assuming 8% WACC, but its true WACC is 12% (because leverage has increased), the NPV turns negative. Third, when deal discipline breaks down in competitive auctions: multiple bidders push price above what any single buyer can justify based on standalone + realistic synergies. The winner's curse is brutal in M&A; the buyer who wins often has overestimated synergies or underestimated integration risk.
