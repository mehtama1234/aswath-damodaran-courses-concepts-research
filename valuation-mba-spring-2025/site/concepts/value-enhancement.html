Concept

Value enhancement

Meaning. Value enhancement identifies actions that increase firm value by changing cash flows, growth, risk, reinvestment, financing, or asset use.

Why it matters. It turns valuation from a passive estimate into an active diagnostic of what management can improve.

How Damodaran develops it. The penultimate session uses value enhancement to connect acquisition closure with the operating levers behind control value.

Common mistakes

  • Calling any restructuring value enhancing without quantifying the lever.
  • Improving accounting optics without improving value.

Worked example

A conglomerate owns three divisions: Oil (20% ROIC), Chemicals (12% ROIC), Retail (8% ROIC). Overall value is $10B at 10% WACC with 5% stable growth. But Retail is dragging: it earns $200M EBIT on $2.5B invested capital (8% ROIC < 10% WACC), destroying value in the core. Value-enhancement option: spin out Retail, which might (a) earn higher ROIC if run independently (12% vs. 8%), (b) find a more appropriate capital structure with lower WACC (8% instead of 10%), and (c) allow the market to price it as a standalone business. Quantifying the improvement: If Retail boosts ROIC to 12% by improving operational efficiency (better inventory turns, pricing power, asset utilization) while maintaining $200M EBIT, the invested capital required drops to $200M / 0.12 = $1.67B. Using perpetuity-based DCF with 8% WACC and 5% growth: Retail value = $200M × 1.05 / (0.08 - 0.05) = $210M / 0.03 = $7.0B. Alternatively, if standalone Retail retains $2.5B invested capital but improves ROIC to 12%, EBIT rises to $300M, and value = $300M × 1.05 / (0.08 - 0.05) = $315M / 0.03 = $10.5B. The first path (capital efficiency) is more realistic for a spinout: the value rises from the original $2.5–3B (at conglomerate WACC) to $7B as a leaner, focused business. The oil and chemicals core, now leaner, might re-rate at a lower WACC (9% instead of 10%) due to reduced complexity. Net value creation: $3–4B, addressable through operational restructuring and proper capital allocation.

Where the approach breaks

Value enhancement fails when management mistakes financial engineering for operational improvement. Example: LBO firms that cut capex to 2% of revenue (half normal levels) can boost short-term FCF and claim value creation, but destroy long-term competitiveness. Within three years, market share erodes, margins fall, and the business becomes worth less. A second failure: integration synergies that never materialize. Post-2008, many bank mergers promised cost synergies of $3–5B but achieved <50%. The value math was sound; the execution was impossible because culture, IT systems, and client conflicts made the savings disappear. Value enhancement is real only when it alters how the business earns returns on capital, not when it rearranges the boxes on an org chart.
