Concept

Riskfree rates

Meaning. The riskfree rate is the base rate in the currency of the cash flows, free of default risk and internally consistent with inflation.

Why it matters. It anchors every discount rate. If this base is wrong, the valuation is contaminated before risk premiums or betas enter.

How Damodaran develops it. Sessions on riskfree rates separate currency choice, sovereign default risk, real versus nominal rates, and maturity matching.

Common mistakes

  • Using a government bond yield mechanically when default risk exists.
  • Using a dollar riskfree rate for non-dollar cash flows.

Worked example

The riskfree rate is the foundation of every discount rate. In USD (US Treasury 10-year ~4.2%), you use 4.2% as your base for a company with nominal cash flows in dollars. A company exposed to Brazilian reais would need Brazil's sovereign rate instead—if Brazil's 10-year yield is 10.5% reflecting inflation expectations and sovereign default risk, that becomes your riskfree rate for real-denominated cash flows.

Example: Tech startup with 5-year explicit forecasts. USD riskfree 4.2%, add equity risk premium 4.6%, multiply by beta 1.10 = Cost of Equity = 4.2% + (1.10 × 4.6%) = 4.2% + 5.06% = 9.26%. This 9.26% discount rate compounds the 4.2% base—if you pick the wrong riskfree rate (say, using a 30-year bond at 4.0% instead), your terminal value swings because that 0.2% difference affects every year forever. The riskfree rate is not overhead; it is the number that shapes every comparison.

Where the approach breaks

Riskfree rates break when sovereign default risk emerges. Argentina's peso 10-year bond yielded 180+ bps over world rates in 2023 because of devaluation and political risk. An analyst treating Argentina's bond yield as 'riskfree' overstates the true riskfree rate for real returns and confuses country risk with genuine safety. The boundary is when the government issuer itself becomes uncertain—use a less distressed peer's rate or build an implied riskfree rate by stripping out the credit spread. Similarly, if inflation expectations spike suddenly (as in 2022), a 10-year nominal riskfree rate at 4.2% today becomes stale in 6 months; the rate must track current expectations, not calendar anchors.
