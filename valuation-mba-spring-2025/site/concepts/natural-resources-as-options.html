Concept

Natural resources as options

Meaning. A natural-resource reserve can behave like an option because the owner can choose when to extract as commodity prices and costs change.

Why it matters. It shows how option logic applies outside technology when the asset has a right to wait and uncertain payoff.

How Damodaran develops it. The natural-resource session uses reserves, commodity prices, extraction cost, and time to develop the option frame.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring extraction cost and reserve life.
  • Treating all commodity exposure as option value.

Worked example

A mining company owns a gold reserve containing 5 million ounces. Current gold price: $2,000/oz. Extraction cost: $1,400/oz. Gross margin: $600/oz × 5M oz = $3B over the life of the mine (assume 10 years of extraction, 500k oz/year). Using straight DCF, the mine is worth roughly $1.5B (present value of $300M annual profit, discounted at 10%). But this ignores the option value. When gold was $1,200/oz (breakeven at current extraction costs), the mine was not worthless—it was an option to wait. If the company extracts at $1,200 gold, it loses money immediately. If it waits 3 years, gold might be $2,400 (and the option to extract is deeply in the money). The option to delay extraction has value because: (1) the company owns the reserve for a fixed period, (2) extraction cost is sunk, (3) commodity prices are volatile. Damodaran's approach: when gold was $1,200, the mine was worth ~$200M (the present value of waiting 2-3 years for higher prices, minus the opportunity cost of not extracting now). When gold hits $2,000, that option value is realized and the mine's total value approaches the DCF value. This logic applies whenever extraction cost is below price and the owner can defer extraction.

Where the approach breaks

The option framework breaks when assumptions about control, timing, or extraction cost collapse. First failure: assuming extraction cost is fixed. In reality, if commodity prices drop 30% and stay there, extraction costs might also fall via lower contractor rates, energy prices, and supply-chain pressure. That 'floor' you thought was $1,400/oz becomes $1,200. The option to wait loses value if the cost advantage erodes. Second failure: ignoring reserve life and depletion. A 50-year reserve has more option value than a 5-year reserve at the same price, because the owner can wait through multiple price cycles. When the reserve depletes to 1 year of production left, the option to wait evaporates—you must extract now or surrender the economics entirely. Third failure: confusing commodity optionality with operational optionality. A single mine with one extraction schedule has an option to defer, but it cannot pivot like a tech startup can. A fourth failure: the company runs out of capital. If the extraction company is cash-constrained and needs the mine to generate cash flow this year, the option to wait is worthless—they will extract no matter the price because they need liquidity. The boundary is crossed when depletion removes waiting time, when costs are volatile enough to erase margin floors, or when capital constraints force immediate extraction regardless of price signals.
