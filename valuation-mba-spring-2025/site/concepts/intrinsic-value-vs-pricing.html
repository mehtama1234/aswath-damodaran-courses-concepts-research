Intrinsic value versus pricing

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Intrinsic value versus pricing

Meaning. Intrinsic valuation asks what an asset is worth from its own cash flows, while pricing asks what similar assets trade for.

Why it matters. The distinction prevents a common analytical category error: using market mood as if it were business value.

How Damodaran develops it. The course first introduces the distinction philosophically, then revisits it in the pricing block with multiples and peer groups.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a multiple-based price an intrinsic value.
  • Using comparables without admitting the claim is relative.

Worked example

Suppose you're valuing Acme Tech, a software company. Using intrinsic valuation, you forecast free cash flows of $10M next year growing at 8% annually for 10 years, with stable 3% perpetual growth thereafter. With a 9% cost of capital, the present value of explicit cash flows = $10M × [1 - (1.08)^10 / (1.09)^10] / (0.09 - 0.08) = $74M. Terminal value = $10M × (1.08)^10 × 1.03 / (0.09 - 0.03) = $195M in year 10, discounted = $82M. Intrinsic value = $74M + $82M = $156 per share (say, on 1M shares).

Now you turn to pricing via peer multiples. Acme's closest peers (Zenith, Nova, Titan) trade at EV/EBITDA multiples of 14x, 15x, and 13x (average 14x). Acme's EBITDA is $12M, so peer pricing = 14 × $12M = $168 per share. The market price is $162. This reveals the danger: if you called the peer multiple "intrinsic value," you would overlook why Acme's intrinsic value is lower. Perhaps Acme's peers are trading at bubble multiples, or Acme has weaker growth. Intrinsic and pricing are different lenses.

Where the approach breaks

The intrinsic-vs-pricing distinction breaks when the analyst conflates the two. In the dot-com bubble (1999–2000), many tech companies had zero profits and negative cash flows, yet multiples soared to 500x revenue. An analyst who based "intrinsic value" on peer multiples would have concluded Pets.com was worth billions; instead, it collapsed. Conversely, in late 2008, financial stocks like Bank of America traded at 0.3x book value and 4x earnings—priced far below intrinsic value by any DCF—because fear and distress spreads depressed the entire sector. The failure boundary is when market prices detach from cash-flow fundamentals (either up in bubbles or down in panics). Relying on pricing alone in such moments destroys capital; using only DCF-intrinsic value, divorced from the fact that nobody can sell at that price, is equally futile. Both approaches are needed: value anchors the long-term story, price reflects what the market will pay today.
