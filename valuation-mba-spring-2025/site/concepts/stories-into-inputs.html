Concept

Stories into inputs

Meaning. Stories matter only when they become model inputs: market size, revenue growth, margins, reinvestment, risk, and terminal economics.

Why it matters. This is how the course turns strategy talk into something that can be tested, debated, and revised.

How Damodaran develops it. The narrative sessions and first valuation exercises force every qualitative claim to show up somewhere in the model.

Common mistakes

  • Letting a persuasive story float above the spreadsheet.
  • Changing inputs without changing the underlying story.

Worked example

A SaaS company tells a story: "We'll grow 40% annually for 5 years in a $30B TAM." That story needs to become model inputs. If current revenue is $50M and target is $50M × (1.40^5) = $604M in year 5, the model must show the market share path: $30B × 2% = $600M. That 2% share assumption is what the 40% growth claim actually means. If margins stay at 20%, FCFF in year 5 = $604M × 20% = $121M.

But the story also carries cost. Revenue growth at 40% requires a sales-to-capital ratio of 0.5x, meaning each $1 of new revenue requires $2 of reinvestment (inventory, receivables, headcount). So year 1 reinvestment = ($50M × 1.4 - $50M) × 2 = $40M. The story drives both the upside (revenue) and the cash demand (reinvestment). Change the story to "TAM is $15B, we take 3% share," and both the numerator and the cash burn change. Numbers flow directly from narrative.

Where the approach breaks

The failure occurs when the story and the numbers decouple. Tesla's growth story worked until Elon Musk claimed 50% CAGR with no mention of where the production capacity and supply-chain investment would come from. Damodaran's constant warning: if your story says "margins expand with scale" but your model shows 50% revenue growth with no increase in CAPEX or working-capital drag, the spreadsheet is lying about the story. The 2008 mortgage boom showed this perfectly—banks told stories of "housing always goes up" but the model inputs were pricing in 0.01% default rates. The story was disconnected from the financial assumptions. When numbers are large enough to justify the story you want, but the story's logic doesn't drive those numbers, you're not analyzing—you're backward-engineering a conclusion.
