Concept

Patents as options

Meaning. A patent can be valued as an option when it gives exclusive rights to commercialize a product if future economics become attractive.

Why it matters. It is a disciplined way to value rights that may be worthless under current cash flows but valuable under future states.

How Damodaran develops it. The patents session applies option logic to technology and drug-development style claims.

Common mistakes

  • Valuing every idea like a patent.
  • Ignoring development cost, expiration, or probability of commercial success.

Worked example

A biotech firm holds a patent for a cancer drug in preclinical stage. Development cost $500M, 10-year timeline, success probability 15%. If successful, peak annual revenue $2B, operating margin 60%, perpetual. The static NPV (expected cash flows) is deeply negative: 0.15 * (3B PV of cash flows) - 500M = -55M. But the patent grants an option to wait, learn, and decide. If early trials show promise, the firm can double investment to accelerate; if trials fail, it abandons. Option value might add $200M: the right to make informed decisions as uncertainty resolves is worth $145M to the stockholder. A standard DCF that sums to -55M would recommend abandoning. The option view says 'the asymmetric payoff—tiny cost of waiting, huge benefit if successful—justifies the patent holder's position.'

Where the approach breaks

Real-options thinking breaks when it becomes cover for wishful accounting. Any uncertain project can be dressed as an 'option'—we pay now, wait for information, then decide. But the discipline of real options requires three things: (1) exclusive or scarce rights (the patent or market position), (2) meaningful flexibility (management can actually adjust the path), and (3) quantifiable payoff asymmetry (upside is materially larger than downside). This fails spectacularly when applied post-hoc to bad investments. Failure example: Merck acquired Medco in 2010 for $40B+ partly on 'option value' reasoning—the combined platform would create data advantages. By 2013, Merck spun it off. The option never materialized because Merck lacked exclusivity or the political will to execute the strategy. Real-options analysis was honest; its application was not. Also breaks: when volatility itself is overestimated. If true success probability is 5%, not 15%, the option value is near zero regardless of the math.
