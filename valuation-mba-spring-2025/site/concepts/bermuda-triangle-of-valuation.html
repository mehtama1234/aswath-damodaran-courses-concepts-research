Bermuda Triangle of valuation

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Bermuda Triangle of valuation

Meaning. The analyst can get lost between story, numbers, and bias unless the valuation process keeps each leg visible and testable.

Why it matters. It gives the course a practical warning: valuation errors are often failures of process rather than failures of arithmetic.

How Damodaran develops it. Early sessions use this frame to separate disciplined valuation from confirmation bias, spreadsheet theater, and market storytelling.

Common mistakes

  • Letting bias choose the assumptions.
  • Using complexity to disguise weak economics.

Worked example

Imagine a biotech startup with a novel cancer drug. Three analysts pick the same growth rate (30% for 5 years) but take different paths to justify it. Analyst 1 (story-driven) reads recent trial press releases and assumes market adoption will follow. Analyst 2 (number-driven) works backward from a $50 billion exit target and backs into required margins. Analyst 3 (bias-driven) has already decided the company is worth $10 billion and adjusts growth to fit. When the drug approval gets delayed, only Analyst 1's written-down story stays honest about what changed. Analyst 2's math still works at lower growth (say 15%) because the numbers were internally consistent. Analyst 3 suddenly calls the delay a 'pause' and shuffles probabilities to hold the valuation. The lesson: write down the story (30% growth because three big hospitals adopt it), show the numbers (3 years to ramp, Year 5 revenue = $2B), and test your bias by asking 'What changes this assumption?' If you can't answer, you have all three legs but no discipline.

Where the approach breaks

The Bermuda Triangle model breaks when the analyst tries to hide inside complexity. A 150-row spreadsheet with cost-of-capital models, sensitivity tables, and Monte Carlo outputs does not save you if the three legs are not clearly written. Real failure: a private-equity team valued a SaaS business at 8x EBITDA (story=stable, predictable), but their spreadsheet had 40% annual churn built in (numbers=doomed). Bias kept the story and numbers separate. Another case: mortgage-backed securities in 2007 had perfect internally consistent math (story=housing always appreciates, numbers=3% default rate built in, bias=nobody questioned the premise). When housing stopped appreciating, the entire system collapsed because the story and numbers had been welded together into one indefensible whole. The boundary is crossed when any leg of the triangle becomes invisible to you or your audience.
