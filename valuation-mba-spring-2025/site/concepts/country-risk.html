Concept

Country risk

Meaning. Country risk captures exposure to macro, political, currency, and default risk beyond mature-market baseline risk.

Why it matters. Global companies are exposed to risk where they operate, not merely where they are incorporated or listed.

How Damodaran develops it. The country-risk sessions connect sovereign spreads, revenue exposure, and implied premiums to the cost of equity.

Common mistakes

  • Assigning country risk by headquarters only.
  • Double-counting country risk in both cash flows and discount rates.

Worked example

Take Petrobras, a Brazilian oil company. Start with the US Treasury yield at 4.5% as a reference. Brazil's sovereign default spread is 2.8% (difference between dollar-denominated Brazil bonds and US Treasuries). So your riskfree rate becomes 4.5% + 2.8% = 7.3%. Now you need an equity risk premium. Global ERP is 5.5%; Brazil's country risk premium adds another 2.2% (derived from the sovereign spread scaled by revenue exposure). Your beta to market risk is 1.15. Cost of equity = 7.3% + (1.15 × 5.5%) + 2.2% = 7.3% + 6.3% + 2.2% = 15.8%. If you wrongly used just the US risk-free rate (4.5%) with a global ERP of 5.5% and same beta of 1.15, you'd get only 4.5% + (1.15 × 5.5%) = 10.8%—and underprice the company by about 48% because you buried the country risk in the cash flows instead of the rate.

The key driver: how much revenue sits in Brazil versus developed markets. A company with 70% Brazil revenue gets hit with most of the country premium. One with 20% Brazil revenue applies the premium only to 20% of cash flows—or the rate goes up by only 0.44% instead of 2.2%.

Where the approach breaks

Country-risk methods break in two directions. First: using sovereign spread directly for every company in the country regardless of revenue exposure or currency match. A Mexican mining company selling in dollars can't use the full Mexico country risk premium; a Mexican retailer selling pesos must. Second failure: double-counting. If you add country risk to the discount rate *and* reduce the terminal growth rate by the same spread, or *and* cut margins for political instability, you've hit the valuation twice. The 2016 Brazil recession and COVID both show this: the sovereign spread blew out to 6%+, but companies with strong dollar revenue and cost advantages (like Natura or JBS) actually held or gained value because their cash flows were partially hedged. Your spreadsheet wasn't; it assumed the worst margin compression everywhere.
