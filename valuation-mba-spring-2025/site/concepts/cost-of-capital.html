Cost of capital

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Cost of capital

Meaning. The cost of capital combines the costs of debt and equity in proportions that match the business and the cash flows being discounted.

Why it matters. It is the firm-wide discount rate for operating cash flows, but only when it is constructed consistently.

How Damodaran develops it. The transition from risk inputs to cash-flow valuation closes with cost of capital before the course turns to cash flows.

Common mistakes

  • Using book-value weights by default.
  • Applying one corporate cost of capital to every asset or geography.

Worked example

A manufacturing firm has 60% equity, 40% debt. Risk-free rate 4.5%, ERP 5%, beta 1.2, cost of debt 5.5%, tax rate 25%. Cost of equity = 4.5% + 1.2 × 5% = 10.5%. After-tax cost of debt = 5.5% × (1 - 0.25) = 4.125%. WACC = (0.60 × 10.5%) + (0.40 × 4.125%) = 6.3% + 1.65% = 7.95%. This 7.95% is the firm-wide hurdle rate for projects and the denominator for discounting FCFF.

If the firm's FCFF is $100M growing at 3%, and terminal growth is 2.5%, terminal value = $100M × 1.025 / (0.0795 - 0.025) = $1,880.73M. That 7.95% rate is critical. If the analyst used 8.5% instead, terminal value = $100M × 1.025 / (0.085 - 0.025) = $1,708.33M—a $172M difference from 55 basis points. The cost of capital drives the discount rate, which drives the terminal value, which usually dominates the DCF.

Where the approach breaks

WACC breaks when the analyst uses book-value weights instead of market-value weights. A highly leveraged firm with a high debt cost will have its WACC pulled down artificially if the analyst weights a $500M book value of equity against current market cap of $2B. Similarly, WACC fails for multi-geography or multi-division firms when applied globally. A US manufacturing business with 40% revenue from emerging markets should not use a uniform 7.95% WACC. During the 2020 COVID crash, many airlines' ratings plunged overnight, cost-of-debt spiked 300+ bps, but CFOs didn't recalculate WACC—they used stale rates. The firm's real hurdle rate changed, but the model didn't, so firms undertook projects that appeared positive under old rates but were value-destructive at true market costs.
