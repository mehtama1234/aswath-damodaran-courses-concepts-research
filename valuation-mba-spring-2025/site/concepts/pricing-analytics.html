Concept

Pricing analytics

Meaning. Pricing analytics uses regressions, screens, and diagnostics to make relative pricing less arbitrary.

Why it matters. It is the course's bridge between informal comparable-company work and a more disciplined market-pricing process.

How Damodaran develops it. The peer-group session shows how to test whether differences in multiples are driven by fundamentals or by market noise.

Common mistakes

  • Letting regression output replace judgment.
  • Ignoring variable definitions and outliers.

Worked example

Suppose you regress EV/EBITDA multiples of 50 retail companies on size (market cap), growth (5-year EBITDA growth rate), and profitability (EBITDA margin). The regression yields: EV/EBITDA = 8.5 + 0.12 × Growth - 0.001 × Size + 1.2 × Margin. For a target company with 10% growth, $5B market cap, and 18% EBITDA margin, the predicted multiple is 8.5 + 0.12(10) - 0.001(5000) + 1.2(18) = 8.5 + 1.2 - 5 + 21.6 = 26.3x. If the firm's EBITDA is $20M, the regression-based price is 26.3 × $20M = $526M. A simple peer-average multiple of 14x would give only $280M—a massive gap. Pricing analytics reveals why: the regression controls for the target's superior growth and margins, justifying a premium.

But the regression is only the starting point. You must check residuals: companies with similar characteristics but trading at 40x are outliers—perhaps they have unique IP or just been hyped. Damodaran's approach demands you go back to the inputs, check variable definitions (did growth include acquisitions or just organic?), and debate whether the regression has built-in selection bias (are mature, low-growth companies underrepresented in your sample?).

Where the approach breaks

Pricing analytics fails when the regression sample is biased or small. During the 2008 financial crisis, regressions built on 2007 data predicted that bank stocks should trade at 0.8x book value and 8x earnings, yet they fell to 0.2x book and 3x earnings as contagion spread. The regression captured normal-time relationships, not tail risk. Second, when variables are endogenous: if you regress growth on multiple and use past growth to predict multiples, you are capturing correlation, not causation. A company with high growth may deserve a high multiple—or it may have high growth only because it is overvalued and burning cash. Third, the regression can hide structural breaks: internet disruption invalidated traditional retail multiples, but regressions built before 2010 would have priced Amazon-era retailers using pre-disruption relationships, destroying value for anyone who followed them.
