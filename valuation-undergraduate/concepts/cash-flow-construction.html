Concept

Cash-flow construction

Meaning. Valuation cash flows are built by translating accounting statements into operating claims after taxes, capex, and working-capital needs.

Why it matters. This is where the course forces students to stop confusing reported earnings with distributable economic cash flow.

How the course develops it. The middle sessions walk through taxes, capex, working capital, and FCFE closure in sequence.

Worked example

A manufacturing company reports net income of $80M on its income statement. But valuation requires free cash flow, not reported earnings. Start with net income: $80M. Add back non-cash charges (depreciation $20M, amortization $5M) = $80M + $25M = $105M. Subtract taxes: actually, you must work backward. Before-tax operating income is $130M (at a 38% effective tax rate, it yields $80.6M net income). Operating cash flow after taxes is $130M × (1 - 0.38) = $80.6M. Now subtract capex (capital expenditures needed to maintain and grow the business): $25M. And adjust for working capital: the company increased inventory by $5M and payables by $2M, a net working-capital increase of $3M (cash outflow). Free cash flow = $80.6M - $25M - $3M = $52.6M. This is the cash actually distributable to all investors (debt and equity). Notice the gap: reported net income was $80M, but true economic cash generation to investors is $52.6M. The difference of $27.4M reflects the cost of maintaining the asset base and funding growth. If you value the company on $80M of earnings instead of $52.6M of cash flow, you overstate value by roughly 50%.

Where the approach breaks

Cash-flow construction fails when capital intensity shifts dramatically or when working-capital changes are temporary windfalls. A retailer like Amazon reported negative free cash flow for years while building warehouses and distribution networks because capex was enormous; investors who valued Amazon on historical net income would have rejected it as "destroying cash," missing that the capex was strategic investment. The opposite trap: a cyclical company like a bank cutting capex to $2M from $12M (because lending is weak) and collecting receivables will show abnormally high free cash flow that looks sustainable but is actually temporary. When working capital swings exceed ±15% of operating cash flow, that year's cash flow is distorted and unusable for valuation—average over three years instead. The most treacherous failure is double-counting: if you use EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization), you must subtract capex and working-capital changes; if you skip that step and apply a simplistic EBITDA multiple, you're valuing phantom cash that doesn't exist. Similarly, startup companies with $0 free cash flow but high growth are almost impossible to value on cash flows alone—the method becomes less reliable as the company transitions from startup to mature.
