Concept

Bermuda Triangle of valuation

Meaning. Students get lost when they confuse value, price, and narrative or let bias control the path between them.

Why it matters. It gives the course a durable warning about category mistakes that recur in investing, company analysis, and market talk.

How the course develops it. The second session names the concept directly, and later storytelling and pricing sessions keep reworking the same distinction.

Worked example

A student valued Company X with 20% revenue growth, 15% EBITDA margins, 12x peer multiple = $500M. Story: AI logistics startup, competitive, strong backers. But three legs failed alignment: (1) Story claimed 20% sustainable via market expansion; market data showed $2B TAM, company at $100M revenue—needing 50 years to reach TAM, making terminal growth drop to 5%. (2) Numbers: at 15% margin, 5% growth, terminal value was 70% of total, requiring only 2% cost-of-capital (impossible). (3) Bias: student was "bullish on AI," accepted 20% without testing reinvestment. At 20% growth, 15% margin: reinvestment = 60-70%, leaving no free cash.

Correct: 8% growth, 12% terminal margin, 35% reinvestment, 9% WACC = $280M, not $500M.

Where the approach breaks

The Bermuda Triangle is hardest to spot because each leg looks reasonable in isolation. Dot-com 2000 had plausible stories (e-commerce grows 100%), spreadsheet precision, and confirmation bias. A practitioner fails when story assumes market share growth math cannot fund (Groupon: 80% growth needs reinvestment impossible at 30% COGS); numbers exclude story reality (WeWork: pro-forma earnings excluded corporate costs, inflating EBITDA 40%); or bias lets weak evidence pass (Theranos: "breakthrough tech" accepted without cash-flow verification). The safest gate: force story → inputs → check backward, not forward.
