Concept

Equity risk premiums

Meaning. The equity risk premium is the market's current price of bearing equity risk over the riskfree alternative.

Why it matters. It is one of the largest levers in any valuation and a direct bridge from market pricing into intrinsic-value inputs.

How the course develops it. The course moves from standard premium intuition into implied premiums and country-sensitive adjustments.

Worked example

In January 2010, after the financial crisis, the equity risk premium was unusually high: investors demanded 6.5% more return for stocks than Treasury bonds (normal historical average is 5-5.5%). By mid-2021, at the peak of the bull market, valuations had expanded significantly. Consider a stock with $5 per share in forward earnings growing at 3%, trading at 20x forward earnings (so the stock price is $100). The dividend yield is $5/$100 = 5%. Using the Gordon Growth Model, the required return is yield plus growth: 5% + 3% = 8%. If the risk-free rate is 1%, then ERP = 8% - 1% = 7%. By contrast, in crisis periods when valuations collapse to 12x earnings, the same $5 stock falls to $60. The yield becomes $5/$60 = 8.33%, so the required return is 8.33% + 3% = 11.33%, implying ERP = 11.33% - 1% = 10.33%. The implication: you cannot use a fixed 5% premium when the market's risk appetite shifts from fear to greed. If you plug a crisis-year 6.5% premium into a bull-market valuation with 7% implied premium, you underestimate equity values. If you plug a bull-market 7% premium into a crisis-recovery valuation where 10% is justified, you overestimate them. The discipline is to either use a long-term historical median (5%), document that you are using it, and acknowledge your precision is low—or to calculate the implied premium quarterly and show how market conditions drive it.

Where the approach breaks

The equity risk premium framework fails when you freeze it in time or apply one country's premium globally. First failure: a 2015 valuation of all US tech stocks using a 5% premium calculated from 1926-2015 data. By 2022, rates had risen 3%, crushing valuations—not because companies got worse, but because the premium was too low at peak multiples. A forward-looking or implied premium would have signaled the danger. Second failure: applying a 5% US premium to a Chinese stock in 2020. China faced political uncertainty, capital controls, and 8-12% inflation. A 7-8% premium was more realistic, but analysts using a global 5% premium grossly overvalued Chinese assets. Third failure: ignoring shifts in investor composition. When retail investors dominate during booms (2000, 2021), risk tolerance is high and premiums compress to 3-4%. When institutions and hedge funds dominate downturns (2008, 2020 March), premiums spike to 7-8% as they reprice tail risk. A static premium misses both. Fourth failure: confusing realized premium (what happened historically) with required premium (what the market demands now). The realized premium from 2000-2010 was 5%, but the required premium in 2001 (when the NASDAQ fell 80%) was 12%. Damodaran's implied-premium framework addresses this. The boundary: if your premium has not been revisited in a year, if it is the same across bull and bear markets, or if it is identical across countries with different risk profiles, you are hiding a critical judgment and calling it a fact.
