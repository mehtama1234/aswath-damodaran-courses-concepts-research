Concept

Asset-based valuation and pricing

Meaning. Asset-based approaches anchor value in underlying assets when operating cash-flow or comparable-company methods become less reliable.

Why it matters. It gives the course a fallback anchor for settings where the default public-company mindset stops working.

How the course develops it. The pricing closure sessions introduce asset-based approaches right where relative pricing reaches its limits.

Worked example

A bank holds $500B in assets on its balance sheet: $300B in loans, $100B in securities, $50B in cash, and $50B in real estate and equipment. Its liabilities are $450B in deposits and $30B in debt, leaving $20B in book equity. For a bank in crisis or during stress testing, operating cash flows are unreliable because lending margins collapse. Asset-based valuation anchors value in the assets themselves: liquidation value. The loans have a face value of $300B but, in a stress scenario, might realize only 85% of value ($255B) due to defaults. The securities (mostly government bonds) trade at $98B due to interest-rate moves. Cash is worth $50B (par). Real estate might fetch $35B in a fire sale (versus $50B at book value). Total liquidation value = $255B + $98B + $50B + $35B = $438B. Subtract liabilities ($480B), and there's a shortfall of $42B—the equity is worthless. This calculation reveals why bank equity collapsed during 2008: asset values, not earnings, were the binding constraint. For a healthy bank, you'd adjust for realistic recovery rates (90% on loans instead of 85%), yielding higher value, but the method is valuable precisely when cash-flow methods are least trustworthy.

Where the approach breaks

Asset-based valuation fails when asset values are intangible, illiquid, or subjective. A software company with $100M in annual revenue has minimal physical assets ($5M in office equipment and servers) but enormous intangible value (brand, code base, customer relationships). The balance sheet shows book equity of $8M, but true value is $800M+; asset-based valuation would severely understate the company. The method also fails in mark-to-market regime shifts: during the 2008 crisis, assets marked at fire-sale prices (35–50 cents on the dollar) looked worthless, but many recovered to par within five years as markets normalized. Lehman Brothers had $639B in assets on its final balance sheet; if you liquidated them in September 2008, you might have realized only $200B due to forced sales and panic. But by 2010, the recovered asset base was worth much closer to book value. The failure threshold is when intangible assets exceed 60% of total assets: at that point, book value is barely related to economic value. Asset-based valuation is most reliable for banks during stress, real-estate companies, and capital-intensive utilities; it is least reliable for tech, pharma (where value is in pipelines), and service businesses.
