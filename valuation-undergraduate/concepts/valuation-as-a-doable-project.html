Concept

Valuation as a doable project

Meaning. The course treats valuation as something students must actually finish: pick a company, build the model, revise it, and make a recommendation.

Why it matters. It is the bridge between theory and practice and one of the clearest differences between this course and a purely conceptual finance sequence.

How the course develops it. From class logistics through live project reminders, the course keeps dragging abstract valuation back into execution discipline.

Worked example

A student picks Microsoft for their class valuation project. Week 1: gather financials, estimate riskfree rate (4.5%), beta (0.95 from regression vs S&P 500), cost of equity = 4.5% + 0.95 × 5.5% = 9.7%. Week 2: project revenue and margins. Microsoft historically 38% operating margin; analyst consensus 40% by year 5 as cloud scales. Working capital stable at 10% of revenue growth. Capex 5% of revenue (lower than gross capex because much is stock-based comp). Week 3: estimate terminal growth at 2.5% (higher than GDP because Microsoft still scaling internationally). Build 5-year model. Year 1 FCFE (free cash flow to equity): Operating income × (1 - tax rate) - Capex + Working capital changes = $50B. Discount at 9.7%. Week 4: terminal value = Year 5 FCFE × 1.025 / (0.097 - 0.025) = $300B. Discount back. Total equity value = $2.5 trillion. Per share = $335. Week 5: current price is $360. Conclusion: *Microsoft is fairly priced or slightly overvalued. The 2% premium doesn't justify risk.*

Then the revision: Microsoft's AI investments (Copilot licensing, Copilot Pro) might expand cloud margins to 45% by year 8. Redo the model: operating income grows faster. New value = $2.7 trillion, or $360/share. Now it's fair. The point: this forced the student to make *executable* claims. Not "AI will change everything"—but *margins rise from 38% to 45% because of pricing power and volume.*

Where the approach breaks

Students fail this by (a) waiting for perfect data, (b) stopping at a number, or (c) building a model they don't understand. Example of (a): spending 6 weeks on historical regression analysis, gathering 20 years of data, when the key driver (terminal growth and terminal margin) is a forecast *guess* anyway. By week 4 of a 7-week project, you need a defensible number, not the perfect number. Example of (b): a student finishes the DCF, gets $85/share, current price $90, says "the stock is overvalued," turns it in. They've done math but not analysis—what specifically must change (higher interest rates, margin compression, faster decline) to make that true? Are those claims defensible? Example of (c): a student copies a peer's model, changes the company name, gets a value, presents it without understanding the mechanics. When the professor asks "if capex is 8% instead of 5%, what happens to terminal value?" they freeze. The real lesson: valuation is *doable* precisely because you pick one company, make explicit assumptions, *defend* them with evidence (guidance, peer comparables, industry trends), revise when data arrives, and write a one-page recommendation that explains why you'd buy or sell. The spreadsheet is the thinking tool, not the answer.
