Overview Thesis Themes Concepts Evidence Sessions

Concept

Private-company valuation

Meaning. Private-company valuation changes when the owners are undiversified, liquidity is limited, and control rights are structured differently from public markets.

Why it matters. It forces the class to confront who owns the asset and under what constraints rather than assuming a public-market marginal investor.

How the course develops it. The private-company session turns owner type, control, and illiquidity into central valuation variables.

Worked example

A family-owned beverage distributor is valued using public comparables. Three public distributors have average cost of equity of 10% (public betas ~1.0, riskfree rate 4%, equity premium 6%). A naive analyst applies 10% to the private firm's FCFF of $5M with 3% perpetual growth, yielding value = $5M × 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) = $73.6M. But the private firm has critical differences: (1) The founder owns 80% with no diversification—she is betting her entire net worth on this business. Her required return should be higher, perhaps 14%, to compensate for undiversification. (2) There is no liquid market; an investor cannot sell shares quickly. Add a 2–3% illiquidity discount. (3) Control structure: all five children have veto rights, creating governance risk absent in public firms. Adjusted cost of equity might be 16%. Value = $5M × 1.03 / (0.16 - 0.03) = $39.6M—46% lower. If an external buyer acquires the firm and can diversify across multiple holdings (or the public market can), the appropriate rate is closer to 10%. Thus, intrinsic value is not fixed; it depends on who owns the firm and under what constraints.

For a buyout offer, a PE firm might use 10% because it will own the firm as part of a portfolio. A family member buying out siblings might justify 14–16% for undiversification premium. Both are internally consistent.

Where the approach breaks

Private-company valuation breaks when the analyst applies a uniform discount to public valuations without diagnosing the real sources of risk. A 20% illiquidity discount is often cited; but why? If the founder is 65 and looking to retire, illiquidity is real and might justify a 30% discount. If a strategic buyer (e.g., a large distributor) can integrate the firm into existing operations, illiquidity vanishes. Conversely, if the firm is a cash cow generating $5M annually with zero growth and zero capital needs, a 20% discount is excessive—the cash can be deployed and risk is low. Second, the method breaks when tax effects are ignored. Private firms often pay lower taxes via owner compensation or discretionary expenses not available to public firms. Applying public-firm tax rates to private FCFF overstates value. If the private firm's true after-tax value is $45M but we adjust for tax inefficiencies that will disappear under new ownership, the buyer's valuation might be $55M. This value transfer is not captured in intrinsic value alone. Third, when key-person risk is ignored: if a $40M private firm's success is entirely dependent on the founder's relationships, and the founder will exit, the buyer faces a cliff in cash flows. Valuations that ignore key-person risk will be too high by 15–30%.
