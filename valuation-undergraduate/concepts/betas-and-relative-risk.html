Concept

Betas and relative risk

Meaning. Beta is used as a way to connect company risk back to market risk, but only after thinking through business mix and leverage.

Why it matters. It prevents discount rates from becoming abstract finance formulas detached from the business being valued.

How the course develops it. The beta sessions push students toward relative-risk reasoning before debt costs and cost of capital are finalized.

Worked example

A conglomerate operates in retail (beta 1.4, 40% of sales) and defense contracting (beta 0.8, 60% of sales). Its regression beta is 1.0, but that masks the mix. Unlevered beta = 0.4 × 1.4 + 0.6 × 0.8 = 0.56 + 0.48 = 1.04 (asset risk). Currently 50% debt-financed (D/E = 1:1), so levered beta = 1.04 × (1 + (1 - 0.25) × 1) = 1.04 × 1.75 = 1.82. If the firm moves to 30% debt, levered beta = 1.04 × (1 + (1 - 0.25) × 0.43) = 1.04 × 1.32 = 1.37. Lower leverage pulls down equity risk. Cost of equity changes from 4% + 1.82 × 6% = 14.9% to 4% + 1.37 × 6% = 12.2%. The beta adjusts because financial risk (leverage) changes, not because operating risk changed. This is why capital-structure decisions matter: they move the beta and the required return.

A private firm in the same retail business has no stock price and no regression beta. Use bottom-up: find public retail comparables (beta 1.3), unlever to 1.0, then relever at the private firm's 40% debt ratio: 1.0 × (1 + (1 - 0.25) × 0.67) = 1.5. Cost of equity = 4% + 1.5 × 6% = 13%.

Where the approach breaks

Beta breaks when you trust a single regression on a firm that changed its business mix. Microsoft's 2014 beta looked like a tech company (high beta), but after Nadella's pivot and Azure revenue scaling, the operating risk profiles shifted—the regression beta lagged reality. Unlevering and relevering assumes you know the right beta for each business unit, which you often don't. A conglomerate with divisions in telecom, insurance, and aerospace that you can't unbundle cleanly will give a misleading bottom-up beta. The other failure is treating financial leverage as a lever on beta when the firm is near distress. Levering beta works when debt is safe; as default probability rises, equity becomes more like a call option, and the linear levering formula (1 + (1-tax rate) × D/E) no longer holds. During the 2008 crisis, banks' betas spiked 2-3x in weeks, but the levering formula predicted smaller moves because it assumes linear risk. Distressed debt breaks the beta framework.
