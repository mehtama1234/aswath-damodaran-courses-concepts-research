Riskfree rates

Overview Thesis Themes Concepts Evidence Sessions

Concept

Riskfree rates

Meaning. The riskfree rate is chosen to match the currency and inflation setting of the valuation, not copied blindly from a default reference bond.

Why it matters. It anchors every later discount-rate choice, so a bad starting rate contaminates the whole model.

How the course develops it. The early rate sessions repeatedly tie the riskfree rate back to currency, sovereign default risk, and inflation logic.

Worked example

A US company generates revenues in dollars and will discount at a dollar riskfree rate: 4.5% (10-year Treasury in 2024). A European company earns in euros; its riskfree rate should be the euro government bond yield (roughly 2.5% in 2024). A company with emerging-market exposure earning in Brazilian reals faces a real riskfree rate of ~8.5% (Brazilian government yield) because inflation in Brazil is higher than in the US. An analyst who used 4.5% for all three would mix currencies in the valuation, breaking cash-flow consistency. The choice of riskfree rate must match: currency of cash flows, inflation expectations embedded in that currency, and the maturity of the bond (typically 10-year government bond for a mature company valuation).

Where the approach breaks

Riskfree rates fail when default risk is present but ignored. In 2015, Venezuela's government bonds yielded 12%, not because Venezuelan investors are that much riskier than Americans, but because default was priced in. Using 12% as a 'riskfree rate' would double-count sovereign default risk in both the rate and the country risk premium. The honest approach: use a near-default-free rate (perhaps from a stable currency like Switzerland or the US) and add country risk explicitly. Another boundary: when inflation expectations shift. From 2019-2022, US Treasury yields were 1-2% while inflation ran 6-8%. A valuation using 1.5% riskfree rate in 2020 was inconsistent with the real growth rates analysts were assuming. By 2023, when riskfree rates had climbed to 4.5%, the same company became worth much less—not because anything about the business changed, but because the discount rate base shifted. Analysts who mechanically apply 'historical average riskfree rates' get caught here.
