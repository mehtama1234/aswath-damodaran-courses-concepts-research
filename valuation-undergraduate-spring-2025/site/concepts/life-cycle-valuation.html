Concept

Life-cycle valuation

Meaning. Firms should be valued differently depending on whether they are young, mature, declining, emerging-market, or financial-service businesses.

Why it matters. It is where the course stops pretending one template can serve every company equally well.

How the course develops it. The life-cycle and special-company sessions test whether the story and assumptions actually fit the business type.

Worked example

A pharma company at year 8 of a blockbuster drug franchise: $2.5B revenue, $800M FCFF, 18% margin. Patent cliff in year 3: generic competition cuts revenue 60% in 18 months post-expiry, margins to 8%, revenue to $1B. Valuation requires two terminal states: (1) Years 1-3: mature growth 3%, WACC 7%, FCFF $800M, PV = 800 × 1.03^3 / 1.07^3 = $714M. (2) Post-cliff (year 4+): generic business, $200M FCFF, 2% growth, WACC 6.5%, terminal value = 200 × 1.02 / 0.045 = $4,533M, PV = $4,533 / 1.07^3 = $3,700M.

Total intrinsic value $4,414M; current market cap $8,000M assumes a new blockbuster pipeline will replace the expiring asset (not guaranteed).

Where the approach breaks

Life-cycle breaks when reinvestment needs are misjudged. Genta (2007): oncology pipeline with 3-4 phase-3 drugs, 35% CAGR claimed, heavy R&D ($120M/year on $50M revenue). But if only 1 of 4 succeeds (25% odds), expected pipeline value was $500M, not $1.5B. Model embedded optimism bias. Second: assuming margin expansion in mature stage. Pharma assumes generics settle 8-12%; but India, China compress to 2-3%, cutting terminal value 70%. Third: ignoring competitive cycle. Mature-stage price competition brutal. Gilead hepatitis C: peak $12.5B revenue (2014), claimed stable 10% margins; by 2022, competition cut revenue to $2B, margins to 5%, repricing 60% down.
