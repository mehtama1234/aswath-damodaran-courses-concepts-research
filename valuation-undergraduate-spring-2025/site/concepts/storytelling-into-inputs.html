Concept

Storytelling into inputs

Meaning. Narrative is allowed only when it changes concrete assumptions about growth, margins, reinvestment, risk, and life-cycle path.

Why it matters. This is how the course allows strategic thinking without letting storytelling float free of economics.

How the course develops it. The storytelling block makes students rewrite qualitative views as model inputs and then defend them.

Worked example

A strategic analyst pitches: 'This company has network effects and will dominate its market.' The pitch is prose. To become a valuation, it must change numbers. Translate: 'Network effects mean customer retention rises from 85% to 93% over 5 years (lower churn)' → margin improves from 8% to 14% as operating leverage kicks in. 'Dominance means revenue growth stays 25% instead of falling to 2%' → sets explicit growth assumptions. Now the story is in the spreadsheet. Year 1: $100M revenue, 8% margin. Year 5: $244M revenue (25% CAGR, calculated as $100M × 1.25^4), 14% margin. Reinvestment: lower at 40% because the business is maturing but still growing. Operating profit in Year 5: $34.2M (14% of $244M). Free cash flow in Year 5: $20.5M (60% of operating profit after 40% reinvestment). Terminal value with 2% perpetual growth: $523M (calculated as $20.5M × 1.02 ÷ 0.06). Compare this to the fade scenario: Year 5 revenue of $108M (2% CAGR), 8% margin, Year 5 operating profit of $8.7M, Year 5 FCF of $5.2M, terminal value of only $133M. The analyst can defend each number by pointing to the story.

Where the approach breaks

Storytelling breaks when the narrative contradicts the numbers in the model. Example: 'This is a commoditizing business losing pricing power' (the story) but the spreadsheet assumes stable 22% gross margins forever (the model). The story and numbers are in conflict. Analyst should rewrite: if commoditization is real, margins must fall from 22% to 18% to 15% over the forecast period. Another boundary: changing numbers without changing the story (arbitraging credibility). If the valuation model suddenly shifts to 30% revenue growth to justify a high stock price, but there is no new story explaining better markets or execution, readers detect the change as opportunistic, not disciplined. The test: read the prose narrative, then read the model. Do they tell the same story numerically?
