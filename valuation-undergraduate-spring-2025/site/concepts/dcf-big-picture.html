Concept

DCF big picture

Meaning. A DCF is the course's organizing architecture for intrinsic value: expected cash flows discounted at rates that match risk, currency, and claim definition.

Why it matters. It turns valuation from a loose idea into an operating framework that can be built and checked piece by piece.

How the course develops it. The course introduces DCF early, then spends most of the semester unpacking the assumptions hidden inside it.

Worked example

Value Apple using DCF framework. Year 1 free cash flow: $110B (operating profit after tax, minus capex, minus working capital increases). Cost of capital: riskfree 4% + beta 1.2 × equity risk premium 5% = 4% + 6% = 10%. Terminal growth: 2% (GDP growth, mature company). Terminal value at end of year 5: assume FCF in year 5 = $130B (grown 4.5% average per year). Terminal value = $130B × 1.02 / (0.10 - 0.02) = $130B × 1.02 / 0.08 = $1.66 trillion. Discount back 5 years: $1.66T / 1.10^5 = $1.03 trillion. Add discounted near-term cash flows: ($110B / 1.10) + ($115B / 1.10^2) + ($120B / 1.10^3) + ($125B / 1.10^4) + ($130B / 1.10^5) = $100B + $95B + $90B + $85B + $80B = $450B. Total equity value = $450B + $1.03T = $1.48 trillion. Divide by 15B shares outstanding: value per share = $98.67.

The architecture: three moving parts. (1) Numerator: cash flows depend on revenue growth and margin discipline. If margins drop from 30% to 25% in year 3 due to competition, cash flows compress and the whole value sinks. (2) Discount rate: if risk rises (beta jumps from 1.2 to 1.5), the denominator grows and value falls 20%. (3) Terminal value: even at 2% growth, it's often 60-70% of total value. If terminal assumptions are optimistic (margin stays at 30% forever when historical decline suggests 28%), the error is baked into that large number.

Where the approach breaks

DCF breaks when the business model is unstable. Nokia in 2007 looked like a DCF dream: $40B revenue, 25% operating margins, stable growth, terminal value accounting for 65% of the valuation. By 2011, iPhone ate the business; margins collapsed to 5%, revenue halved. A 2007 DCF-based value per share of €40 crashed to €2 because the underlying assumptions (smartphone dominance, margin stability) were wrong. The lesson: terminal value is only as good as the business story. If the market structure (competition, technology, regulation) is fragile, claiming 2% terminal growth for 30+ years is dangerous. Second failure: using historical cash flows directly as a baseline. Kodak in 1995 had incredible free cash flow ($3B on $15B revenue); the film business was defensible *then*. But if you projected those cash flows forward unchanged, digital disruption erased 90% of value. The DCF framework is sound, but it requires honest assumptions about when competitive advantages erode, not just regressions on historical margins.
