Growth and reinvestment

Overview Thesis Themes Concepts Evidence Sessions

Concept

Growth and reinvestment

Meaning. Growth creates value only when the business can fund it through credible reinvestment economics.

Why it matters. It stops students from treating growth as free upside and ties the valuation back to operating reality.

How the course develops it. Growth sessions keep connecting expansion to reinvestment needs, margins, and return logic.

Worked example

A fast-growing e-commerce platform projects 25% revenue growth and reinvests $150M annually out of $200M operating cash flow, a 75% reinvestment rate. To sustain 25% growth at 75% reinvestment requires ROIC = 25% / 0.75 = 33.3%, but the platform only achieves 10% ROIC in practice. The gap is stark: the business is reinvesting at levels that would need 33% returns to justify the growth, yet earning only 10%. This mismatch exposes value destruction. The intrinsic growth the reinvestment can actually support is only 0.75 × 0.10 = 7.5%, far below the claimed 25%. Either the 25% growth projection is unsustainable (requiring external financing to supplement the 7.5% organic growth), or the ROIC must rise dramatically to justify the reinvestment. Meanwhile, if cost of equity is 12% (above the 10% ROIC), every dollar reinvested at 10% returns destroys value. The student must conclude: either (a) ROIC must climb to at least 12% to break even, and to 33% to justify the reinvestment at 25% growth, or (b) growth must be cut to 7.5% or below if ROIC stays at 10%, or (c) the company is a value trap. This forces discipline: growth without credible reinvestment returns is a mirage.

Where the approach breaks

Growth-and-reinvestment logic breaks when disruption reshapes returns suddenly. Smartphone makers saw ROIC collapse from 20% (2007) to 8% (2016) as competition intensified and capex needs rose for leading-edge fabs. Models built on historical 15% ROIC crashed. Second failure: platform businesses where reinvestment transitions from high to low. Uber's reinvestment rate was 90%+ in growth phase (ROIC 5%), but as it matured, reinvestment fell to 30% and ROIC rose to 15%. Simple linear models miss this transition. Third: emerging-market currencies. A high-growth Indian software firm might earn strong rupee ROIC, but currency depreciation erodes dollar ROIC by 30% even if operations are sound. Reinvestment discipline requires constant stress-testing against disruption, transition, and currency headwinds.
