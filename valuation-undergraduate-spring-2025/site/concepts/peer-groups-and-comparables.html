Concept

Peer groups and comparables

Meaning. Relative pricing depends on which firms are judged similar enough to act as comparables and how those similarities are defined.

Why it matters. This is where pricing becomes either disciplined or manipulable depending on peer-group judgment.

How the course develops it. The peer-group analytics session makes the comparable set itself part of the analysis rather than a background assumption.

Worked example

You are valuing a regional specialty retailer with EBITDA of $80M and net income of $32M. You identify six comparable public retailers in the same niche. Their EV/EBITDA multiples are: 7.2x, 8.1x, 8.5x, 8.9x, 9.2x, and 10.1x. The median is 8.7x. Applied to your target's EBITDA of $80M, this yields an enterprise value of $696M. Dividing by shares outstanding of 40M gives an implied value per share of $17.40.

But the peers differ in key ways. Four of the six trade at 8.5x or below; they are mature, low-growth retailers with steady margins of 12-14%. Two trade at 9.2x and 10.1x; they are faster-growing (8-10% annually) with improving margins. Your target has grown 6% recently with 11% margins, placing it between the two clusters. Adjusting the median: the slower group's implied value is $688M, the faster group's is $736M. A 6% growth rate and 11% margins suggest a slight discount to the fast group and a premium to the slow group, so a reasonable estimate is $710M, or $17.75 per share. This is higher than the raw median and reflects that your firm is slightly above the median in quality but below the fastest growers.

Where the approach breaks

Peer-group multiples break down when the set mixes firms with different growth rates, profitability drivers, or capital structures without explicit adjustment. A pharmaceutical company trading at 18x EV/Sales because it owns high-margin intellectual property cannot be compared directly to a generics manufacturer at 3x EV/Sales without accounting for patent cliffs, R&D intensity, and return on capital. Similarly, comparables fail when peers serve different customer bases or markets with different competitive intensity—a retailer with physical moats is not interchangeable with an online pure-play just because both are labeled retail. The method also conceals leverage differences: two firms at identical EV/EBITDA multiples may have very different debt loads, making one riskier than the other. Finally, peer groups become manipulable if the analyst cherry-picks comps to support a preconceived answer, treating the median as a fact rather than as evidence subject to judgment about which firms are truly comparable.
