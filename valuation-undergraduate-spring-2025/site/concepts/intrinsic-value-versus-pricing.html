Overview Thesis Themes Concepts Evidence Sessions

Concept

Intrinsic value versus pricing

Meaning. Intrinsic value asks what the asset is worth from its own cash flows, while pricing asks what similar assets trade for in a market.

Why it matters. This separation is one of the course's most durable intellectual distinctions and a major protection against analytical confusion.

How the course develops it. The transition into pricing is explicit: Damodaran treats it as a different language, not a lighter version of DCF.

Worked example

Intrinsic value: Ford in 2023. Project forward 10 years of cash flows: $5B → $6B → $7B (modest growth as EV scales), terminal value year 10 = $7B × 1.02 / (0.09 - 0.02) = $100B. Discount all back at 9% cost of capital: sum of discounted flows = $48B. Divide by 4.2B shares: intrinsic value per share = $11.40. That's what the cash flows *are worth to Ford in isolation*. Now pricing. Ford trades at $10.50. Why the gap? Pricing logic is different: peer group is GM (trading at 0.6× book), Tesla (12× sales), Toyota (1.1× book). Ford at $10.50 = 0.7× book value. The *market price* reflects Ford's risk relative to peers (weaker balance sheet than Toyota, less EV juice than Tesla), not its isolated cash flows. A multiple-based investor says: Ford trades at 0.7× book; historical average is 0.8×, so it's slightly cheap. That's a pricing conclusion, not an intrinsic-value conclusion. Intrinsic value says: if Ford's FCF hits $7B and stays stable, it's worth $11.40. Pricing says: peers trade here, and Ford's risks (legacy costs, chip shortage exposure) warrant a discount to Toyota.

The difference drives investment: intrinsic-value analyst buys at $10.50 expecting $11.40, waits. Pricing analyst asks: will the peer group re-rate Ford upward? If market sentiment swings and Ford's multiple goes from 0.7× to 0.9× book (because EV transition looks successful), price jumps to $13.50 *without* underlying cash flows changing. That's a pricing trade, not a value bet.

Where the approach breaks

This separation breaks when you forget the market sets price. A student valuing Tesla in 2021 with intrinsic-value logic: assume 2 million cars by 2030, $15k margin per car, 15% EBITDA margin on services = $5B+ annual FCF. Discount at 8% (low risk) to get $60B value, suggest $600/share. Price was $900+. Mistake: ignoring pricing context. Tesla's pricing reflects (a) volatility premium (Tesla is *risky*, beta 2+, not 0.9), (b) growth optionality (2 million cars might mean 10 million by 2040), (c) market sentiment (tech darling, Musk premium). Using 8% cost of capital was the error—should have used 12-15% to account for single-stock risk and technological uncertainty. Even with higher rates, the premium gap suggests pricing factors (peer sentiment, momentum) that intrinsic value alone cannot capture. Reverse failure: a 2015 Blackberry analyst using pricing logic (compare to iPhone multiple, apply a haircut) misses the *intrinsic* story—Blackberry's cash flows were evaporating and terminal value was near zero. No multiple adjustment could save the investment. The key: intrinsic value protects you from overpaying for a pricing narrative that turns; pricing alerts you when intrinsic value is *already* built in the multiple and you need momentum or sentiment to win.
