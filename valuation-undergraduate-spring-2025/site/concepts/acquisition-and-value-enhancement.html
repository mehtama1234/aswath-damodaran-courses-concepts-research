Concept

Acquisition and value enhancement

Meaning. Acquisition value depends on who can change decisions, create synergies, and improve the business after control changes hands.

Why it matters. It turns valuation from passive measurement into a framework for governance, operating change, and strategic intervention.

How the course develops it. The course ends by linking acquisition cases to value enhancement and decision rights rather than stopping at stand-alone valuation.

Worked example

Public Co. A (market cap $10B) targets Private Co. B (EBITDA $150M, fair value $900M or 6x). Acquirer pays $1.2B (8x), assuming $200M synergies: $80M COGS reduction, $60M head-count, $60M revenue. At 8% discount, PV synergies over 5 years (full run-rate year 3) = $750M. Deal: $1.2B - $900M = $300M premium, supported by $750M value, leaving $450M value creation.

But if head-count synergies prove 30% harder ($42M vs $60M), synergy PV drops to $700M, and deal adds only $400M value. Post-integration often realizes 60-70% of claimed synergies due to cultural friction, churn, and delays.

Where the approach breaks

Acquisition fails when synergy assumptions are one-way. HP/Compaq (2002, $19B): claimed $2.5B synergies; realized only $1.8B before integration costs soared, destroying $60B market cap. Revenue synergies assumed customer sticky-ness that didn't materialize. AOL/Time Warner (2000, $164B): content/distribution synergies evaporated. Second: purchase-price allocation tax drag. Allocating $400M to goodwill at 8x versus 6x intrinsic means no tax shields over 15 years; $400M × 30% = $120M NPV reduction. Third: acquirer operations stall during integration. If acquirer grows 10% absent deal, drops to 6% during 2-year integration, that 4% gap on $100B revenue = $4B destruction, often exceeding stated synergies.
