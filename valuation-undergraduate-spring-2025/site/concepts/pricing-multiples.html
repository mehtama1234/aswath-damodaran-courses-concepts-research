Concept

Pricing multiples

Meaning. Multiples compress assumptions about growth, risk, and profitability into a market ratio that looks simple only because the assumptions are hidden.

Why it matters. It helps students see why comparables are powerful in markets but still require disciplined interpretation.

How the course develops it. The pricing sessions unpack the hidden drivers inside common multiples rather than treating them as plug-and-play facts.

Worked example

Consider two retailers, both earning $100M net income. Retailer A trades at 20x P/E ($2B market cap), Retailer B at 10x P/E ($1B market cap). Same earnings, wildly different multiples. Why? Retailer A is growing 12% annually with 20% net margins and a 15% ROIC. Retailer B is growing 3% with 12% net margins and a 10% ROIC. To value these, use the formula: P/E = [1 - g/ROIC] * (1 + g) / (r - g), where r is cost of equity and g is growth rate. This connects growth to the reinvestment needed: the payout ratio is 1 - (g/ROIC). For Retailer A: payout ratio = 1 - (0.12/0.15) = 0.20 (20% of earnings paid out, 80% reinvested to fuel 12% growth). P/E = 0.20 * 1.12 / (0.10 - 0.12) is undefined because cost of equity is below growth (typical for high-growth firms), so use a different framing: the 20x multiple is justified if the firm can sustain 12% growth with 15% ROIC and investors require 10% return. For Retailer B: payout ratio = 1 - (0.03/0.10) = 0.70 (70% paid out). P/E = 0.70 * 1.03 / (0.10 - 0.03) = 0.70 * 1.03 / 0.07 ≈ 10.3x. This matches the 10x multiple. The multiple hides these assumptions: Retailer A's higher multiple reflects higher growth, better ROIC relative to growth needs, and willingness to reinvest. Retailer B's lower multiple reflects lower growth and higher payout because reinvestment needs are smaller. A mechanical 'apply the 15x median' to a new retail target is silent about what growth and ROIC profile you are assuming.

Where the approach breaks

Multiples fail when the analyst ignores comparability. A high-growth SaaS firm at 40x revenue looks expensive versus a mature software firm at 6x revenue. But SaaS has 70% net margins and 50% ARR growth with 25% ROIC; mature software has 35% net margins and 5% growth with 18% ROIC. For SaaS: payout = 1 - (0.50/0.25) is negative (reinvestment needs exceed earnings), so the entire profit is reinvested and more, creating a high multiple. For mature software: payout = 1 - (0.05/0.18) ≈ 0.72, supporting a much lower revenue multiple. At constant discount rates, 40x revenue and 6x revenue are both defensible, but only if you understand the ROIC and reinvestment hidden in them. Real failure: applying EV/EBITDA to capital-intensive versus asset-light businesses. An EV/EBITDA of 12x for a telecom (capex 20% of revenue, long asset lives) is very different from 12x for a software firm (capex 3% of revenue). The reinvestment burden is embedded in ROIC; two firms with the same EV/EBITDA but different ROICs have different true returns to investors. Multiples also break when accounting definitions differ. US GAAP and IFRS can produce different EBITDA figures for the same business; using a multiple on one definition and the other's earnings is inconsistent. Extreme case: in 2007-2008, financial stocks traded at 1.5-2x book value shortly before the crisis. The multiple was capturing pricing from a regime that no longer existed—historical value drivers (steady deposits, predictable loan spreads) evaporated. Multiples are backward-looking; they break when the future is discontinuous.
