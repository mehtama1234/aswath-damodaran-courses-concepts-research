Concept

Pricing multiples

Meaning. Multiples compress assumptions about growth, risk, and profitability into a market ratio that looks simple only because the assumptions are hidden.

Why it matters. It helps students see why comparables are powerful in markets but still require disciplined interpretation.

How the course develops it. The pricing sessions unpack the hidden drivers inside common multiples rather than treating them as plug-and-play facts.

Worked example

Consider two retailers, both earning $100M net income. Retailer A trades at 20x P/E ($2B market cap), Retailer B at 10x P/E ($1B market cap). Same earnings, wildly different multiples. Why? Retailer A is growing 15% annually with 18% operating margins and reinvesting at 8% of revenue. Retailer B is growing 3% with 12% margins and reinvesting 15% of revenue. The multiple formula: P/E = (1 + g) * (Net Margin) / (Cost of Equity - g). Higher growth and better margins justify the premium. Retailer B's lower multiple reflects lower growth and higher reinvestment needs (less cash to shareholders). The multiple hides these assumptions; a mechanical 'apply the 15x median' to a new retail target is silent about what growth and margin profile you are assuming.

Where the approach breaks

Multiples fail when the analyst ignores comparability. A high-growth SaaS firm at 40x revenue looks expensive versus a mature software firm at 6x revenue. But SaaS has 70% margins and 50% ARR growth; mature software has 35% margins and 5% growth. At constant discount rates, 40x revenue and 6x revenue are both defensible, but only if you understand what's hidden in them. Real failure: applying EV/EBITDA to capital-intensive versus asset-light businesses. An EV/EBITDA of 12x for a telecom (capex 20% of revenue, long asset lives) is very different from 12x for a software firm (capex 3% of revenue). Multiples also break when accounting definitions differ. US GAAP and IFRS can produce different EBITDA figures for the same business; using a multiple on one definition and the other's earnings is inconsistent. Extreme case: in 2007-2008, financial stocks traded at 1.5-2x book value shortly before the crisis. The multiple was capturing pricing from a regime that no longer existed—historical value drivers (steady deposits, predictable loan spreads) evaporated. Multiples are backward-looking; they break when the future is discontinuous.
