Concept

Terminal value discipline

Meaning. Terminal value is the mature-state closure of the valuation and therefore a test of whether the whole story is internally consistent.

Why it matters. Because terminal value can dominate the result, small mistakes here can overwhelm the rest of the model.

How the course develops it. The terminal-value sessions isolate the idea and show how stable growth, reinvestment, and risk have to fit together.

Worked example

A 10-year explicit-forecast DCF values a high-growth firm with 20% growth over years 1-10, then assumes 3% perpetual growth thereafter. FCFF in year 10 is $500M; cost of capital is 8%. The terminal value is 500M × 1.03 / (0.08 - 0.03) = $10.3B—representing 78% of the firm's total value. If the analyst misses by just 0.5% on terminal growth (choosing 3.5% instead of 3%), the terminal value becomes 500M × 1.035 / (0.08 - 0.035) = $11.4B, a 10% increase in total firm value despite a half-percentage-point change in one assumption that is itself uncertain. A more disciplined approach: match terminal growth to long-term GDP growth (~2-2.5% in developed markets) and verify that terminal ROIC does not exceed WACC. If the firm forecasts 4% terminal growth but declining ROIC (say, 9% versus 8% WACC), value destruction is built into the mature-state assumptions—an internal contradiction.

Second example: terminal value as a sanity check. If the firm should stabilize at $600M annual FCFF in year 10 and a 2% terminal growth rate, the terminal value is 600M × 1.02 / (0.08 - 0.02) = $10.2B. That corresponds to a 17× multiple on year-10 cash flows, high but defensible only if the firm is a fortress-quality operator (minimal reinvestment needs, stable competitive position). If the same firm is capital-intensive or faces disruption, 17× is suspect, and the analyst should lower terminal growth or increase risk premium, bringing terminal value down until it is credible.

Where the approach breaks

Terminal value fails when growth assumptions are inconsistent with reinvestment and returns. If a firm is projected to grow 4% in perpetuity but requires 5% reinvestment intensity (capital invested / FCFF) to hit that growth, and earns only 7% ROIC, then growth is destroying value at a rate of (ROIC - WACC) × reinvestment = (0.07 - 0.08) × 0.05 = -0.5% annually in value destruction. Historically, the biggest valuation failures happened when terminal value was allowed to rescue an implausible near-term story. Pets.com (1999) projected high early growth but never reached profitability; analysts used terminal growth of 5%+ for a retail pet-products business, no matter how weak early economics looked. General Electric, on the other hand, became dangerously indebted chasing growth; its terminal-value assumptions implied 4-5% growth into perpetuity on a diversified industrial firm, not credible as markets matured. The hard boundary is: if terminal ROIC < WACC, growth is not sustainable, and terminal value should reflect that through a declining value formula or explicit run-off.
