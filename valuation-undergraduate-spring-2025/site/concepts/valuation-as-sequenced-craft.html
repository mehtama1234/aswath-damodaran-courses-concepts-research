Concept

Valuation as sequenced craft

Meaning. Valuation is taught as a learnable sequence of choices rather than a mysterious expert ritual.

Why it matters. This is the pedagogical spine of the undergraduate course and the reason the material feels executable rather than ornamental.

How the course develops it. The early sessions keep breaking valuation into ordered steps, then the later sessions return to that sequence when the class reaches pricing, options, and acquisitions.

Worked example

Valuation is a learnable sequence: (1) define the business story, (2) translate it into model inputs (growth, margins, reinvestment, risk), (3) build cash flows, (4) pick a discount rate, (5) calculate intrinsic value, (6) compare to market price. A student analyzing a fast-growing SaaS company follows the sequence: story = "vertical software for warehouses, 40% TAM expansion, unit economics proven, needs to scale ops." Inputs from story: Year 1-5 revenue growth 35%, operating margin 5% → 20%, reinvestment at 60% of incremental revenue growth, tax rate 25%, risk (beta 1.4, risk-free rate 4%, market risk premium 4.6%). Calculate: Cost of equity = 4% + 1.4 × 4.6% = 10.44%, rounded to 10.5%. Year 1 revenue $100M. Year 1 EBIT = $100M × 5% = $5M. Year 1 EBIT after tax = $5M × (1 - 0.25) = $3.75M. Year 1 revenue growth in dollars = $100M × 35% = $35M. Year 1 reinvestment = $35M × 60% = $21M. Year 1 FCFF = $3.75M - $21M = -$17.25M (negative because growth investment exceeds near-term cash generation; this is realistic for early-stage SaaS). By Year 5, as margins expand to 20% and growth slows, EBIT after tax = ($100M × 1.35^5) × 20% × (1 - 0.25) ≈ $448.4M × 0.15 ≈ $67.3M, and reinvestment drops as growth decelerates. Discount years 1-5 FCFF at 10.5% cost of equity. Terminal value using stable growth 3%: Terminal FCFF in Year 6 ≈ $67.3M × 1.03. Terminal Value = ($67.3M × 1.03) / (0.105 - 0.03) ≈ $924M. PV of terminal value at 10.5% over 5 years ≈ $561M. Sum PV of forecast years + PV of terminal value → intrinsic enterprise value ≈ $450M. Market cap $200M → potential upside, but only if story holds.

Each step forces a choice: Is 35% growth sustainable? Does 60% reinvestment ratio match the story (heavier upfront, then lighter as maturity nears)? Why does margin expand to 20%? This discipline separates valuation from guessing.

Where the approach breaks

The sequence breaks when students inherit a spreadsheet without understanding which story each number came from. They adjust cells to match a desired price, breaking the link between narrative and model. Red flag: if you change revenue growth but the margin assumptions don't change to match the new story (e.g., higher growth but same low margins → impossible reinvestment), you have abandoned the sequence. Another boundary: if the explicit forecast period inputs look plausible (say, 20% growth) but the terminal value assumes 50% of today's net income forever, the story is broken. A third critical boundary: if you calculate FCFF using the formula EBIT × (1 - Tax) - Reinvestment (in dollar terms), but then apply reinvestment as a percentage multiplier on margin, you have mixed incompatible rate concepts and broken the cash flow logic. The sequence works only when every input answers the question: 'Why does the story require this number?'
