Overview Thesis Themes Concepts Evidence Sessions

Concept

Cost of capital

Meaning. The cost of capital combines debt and equity costs into a rate that matches operating cash flows.

Why it matters. It is where the logic of rates, risk, and claims has to come together consistently.

How the course develops it. The course closes the discount-rate block by building the cost of capital before moving to cash-flow definition.

Worked example

Consider a firm with market value of equity $300M and market value of debt $100M. The cost of equity (from CAPM) is 10%, and the cost of debt (YTM on bonds) is 5%. The marginal tax rate is 30%. WACC = (E / (E+D)) × Cost of Equity + (D / (E+D)) × Cost of Debt × (1 - Tax Rate) = (300/400) × 10% + (100/400) × 5% × (1 - 0.30) = 0.75 × 10% + 0.25 × 5% × 0.70 = 7.5% + 0.875% = 8.375%. This 8.375% is the rate at which to discount the firm's free cash flows. If FCFF next year is $40M with 5% perpetual growth, Firm Value = $40M × 1.05 / (0.08375 - 0.05) = $40M × 1.05 / 0.03375 = $1,244M.

The beauty of WACC: it reflects both the firm's operating risk (beta, embedded in cost of equity) and its financial structure (the D/E ratio and tax shield). If the firm were 100% equity-financed, WACC would be 10%. Leverage lowers WACC to 8.375% because debt is cheaper and interest is tax-deductible. The analyst must use market values, not book values (book D/E often misses intangible assets and may include stale debt), and must ensure the tax rate is marginal, not average.

Where the approach breaks

Cost of capital breaks when the analyst assumes a firm's capital structure is stable when it is not. During a recession, a firm's equity value may halve (from $300M to $150M) while debt stays at $100M. Using the old WACC of 8.375% continues to discount cash flows as if the capital structure is 75% equity / 25% debt, but it is now 60% / 40%. The true WACC is higher, and firm value is lower than the model suggests—a trap many leveraged buyout analysts fall into when assuming stable structure through an economic downturn. Second, WACC assumes the firm's debt is safe and the cost of debt reflects only credit spread; if the firm is near distress, the cost of debt will spike and equity holders will demand a higher return because they expect losses. Using a historical cost of debt (e.g., 5%) when the firm is now stressed is dangerous. Third, WACC is a blunt instrument for multi-division firms: a conglomerate with both stable utilities and volatile tech divisions cannot use a single WACC credibly; divisional WACCs are essential.
