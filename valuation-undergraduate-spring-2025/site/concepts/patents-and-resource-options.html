Concept

Patents and resource options

Meaning. Patents and natural resources make option logic visible because the owner controls timing over an uncertain but exclusive economic right.

Why it matters. These cases make contingent upside easier to reason about than standard operating-company examples do.

How the course develops it. The course uses patents and resource rights as the cleanest teaching cases for real-options intuition.

Worked example

A pharmaceutical firm holds a patent on a drug with 8 years of remaining protection. Standard DCF values the drug at $500M based on expected cash flows. But the patent grants an option: if clinical trials for a follow-on indication succeed (50% probability), the firm can extend the franchise to a $200M additional market, adding $80M in PV. A simple DCF would miss this optionality entirely, recording only the $500M base value. An options lens adds: (0.50 × $80M) = $40M in option value, for a total of $540M. The difference is not large here, but it is real and reflects the firm's exclusive control over both the original market and the probability of expansion.

A natural-resources example: an oil driller holds a lease on undeveloped acreage with proved reserves worth $30 per barrel today. Development requires $2B capex. A DCF at current oil prices ($70/barrel) yields NPV = -$500M—a bad project. But the firm holds an option to wait: if oil prices rise to $85, the project becomes $1B NPV and suddenly rational. The value of waiting—the option to abandon if oil collapses or to expand if it surges—is typically 10-15% of the risked DCF value for long-lived, high-volatility assets. Ignoring it systematically undervalues natural-resource companies.

Where the approach breaks

Real options fail when analysts invoke them as ad-hoc value salvagers. Every uncertain project is not an option; true options require (1) managerial control over the timing and scale decision, (2) irreversibility or high exit cost (once deployed, the capital is locked in), and (3) meaningful uncertainty that creates value through waiting or flexibility. A manufacturing plant facing uncertain demand is often an option to expand or contract. A 5-year research project with a gate decision at year 3 is an option. A commodity business that simply faces price volatility is not an option—it is a risky project that belongs in a risk-adjusted DCF. The boundary is crossed when 'the market does not understand our option value' becomes an excuse for a low trading multiple. General Electric's long-running claim that investors undervalued its 'real options' embedded in power-generation and healthcare divisions often masked capital inefficiency, not hidden option value. Patents are real options only when the firm can credibly choose to develop or license; if development is forced by competition, there is no optionality.
