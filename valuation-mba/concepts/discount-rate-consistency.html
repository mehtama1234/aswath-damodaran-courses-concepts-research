Discount-rate consistency

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Discount-rate consistency

Meaning. The discount rate must be built in the same currency, inflation terms, claim definition, and risk frame as the cash flows.

Why it matters. It is the guardrail that keeps DCF from becoming a collection of copied market inputs.

How Damodaran develops it. Riskfree-rate, ERP, country-risk, beta, and cost-of-capital sessions all return to this matching principle.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing currencies.
  • Applying a firm discount rate to equity cash flows.

Worked example

A firm with international operations earns revenues in euros and dollars. The analyst builds a DCF in euros, forecasting 50M euro cash flows growing at 2% annually, but mistakenly discounts using a 9.5% dollar-denominated cost of capital. The result conflates two different risk frames: the valuation immediately becomes incoherent. By switching to a 6.8% euro-denominated WACC (built from a 1.2% euro riskfree rate, a 4.2% euro-adjusted equity risk premium, and a 1.1 beta), the terminal value becomes 50M×1.02/(0.068-0.02) = 1,062.5M euro. The swap between dollar and euro rates, reflecting different inflation expectations, changes the valuation by roughly 25% just by restoring consistency.

Another example: a private utility generating US dollar cash flows of 100M annually at a 7% growth rate faces the question of hurdle rate. If the analyst uses a 9% WACC (US-based) but then applies it to real cash flows (inflation-adjusted), the discount rate and cash flow work in different inflation worlds, producing a terminal value that overstates value by 5-8% depending on inflation assumptions.

Where the approach breaks

Discount-rate consistency breaks most visibly when firms operate across currencies or when valuation mixes real and nominal inputs carlessly. A firm issuing euro-denominated debt but earning dollar cash flows without hedging faces currency-rate risk; if the model discounts everything at a dollar WACC while forecasting nominal euro growth, the analysis has already hidden a currency mismatch that real investors cannot ignore. The boundary is sharpest when leverage changes: a firm moving from zero debt (equity cost of equity 10%) to 40% debt financing should see its cost of equity drop (less than 10%) and its WACC fall too. If instead the analyst uses 10% equity cost for both scenarios and just reweights debt and equity, the discount rate does not reflect the changed risk of residual equity—a 3-4 percentage point error that dominates small valuations.
