Concept

Growth estimation

Meaning. Growth has to be estimated from fundamentals, analyst judgment, past performance, and the operating story rather than assumed as an isolated input.

Why it matters. Growth is often the most seductive valuation input, and the course makes it earn its way into the model.

How Damodaran develops it. The growth block links revenue growth, margins, reinvestment, return on capital, and life-cycle stage.

Common mistakes

  • Projecting high growth without asking how it is funded.
  • Extrapolating history when the business is changing.

Worked example

A retail chain earned $50M in operating cash flow last year. If you assume 12% annual growth for the next decade without justifying it, you're gambling. Instead, estimate growth from fundamentals: the company has 500 stores, each generating $100K in annual cash. To grow 12%, it must either add 60 stores per year (which requires real capex and market availability) or increase same-store cash flow by 12% (requiring pricing power or cost discipline). Checking real data: same-store sales grew only 2% last year, and new-store capex budgets suggest 40 stores per year maximum. This yields actual growth of roughly (40 / 500) × 1% (store-count contribution) + 2% (same-store improvement) = 2.8% at best. That's half your assumption. Or look backward: the company's growth over the past decade averaged 4%, and industry maturity is reducing it. Analyst consensus is 5% forward. Your base-case should start at 5% with clear operating reasons for any variance—more pricing power, cost reductions, or geographic expansion into underserved markets.

Growth estimation also requires matching growth to reinvestment. If a company grows 20% and reinvests only 10% of earnings, where is the growth coming from? It must be drawing down working capital or harvesting underutilized assets—unsustainable. A mature company growing 3% might reinvest 3% of earnings (because new assets barely exceed depreciation), while a young software company growing 25% needs to reinvest 30% or more to handle scaling. Building the growth estimate this way—from capex budgets, headcount plans, and market share logic—forces discipline.

Where the approach breaks

Growth estimation fails spectacularly when structural shifts are invisible to backward-looking data. Kodak dominated photography for 80 years with stable 5% growth, then digital disruption rendered the business model obsolete in a decade. Netflix grew at 30%+ annually for 15 years because streaming was a new category, but as it matured and competition arrived, growth collapsed toward 5%—an 80% deceleration in a few years. Startups are the worst case: if a company has no history, analyst consensus is guesswork, and the addressable market is speculative, the growth rate is essentially a wild guess. The failure threshold is particularly acute when terminal growth exceeds 2–3%: assuming a company will grow faster than GDP in perpetuity is almost always wrong unless it is a defensive monopoly (like Nestlé) with secular tailwinds. Be especially skeptical of growth estimates for companies in industries that are consolidating (telecom) or being disrupted (print media)—historical growth extrapolation is a trap.
