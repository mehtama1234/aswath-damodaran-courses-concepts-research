Distressed equity

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Distressed equity

Meaning. Equity in a deeply distressed firm can resemble an option on the firm's assets after debt claims are considered.

Why it matters. It explains why equity can retain value even when conventional DCF equity value looks close to zero.

How Damodaran develops it. The distressed-equity session uses option logic to value residual claims when debt creates asymmetric payoffs.

Common mistakes

  • Treating distressed equity like normal equity.
  • Ignoring debt maturity, asset volatility, and claim priority.

Worked example

An airline with $3B in assets, $2B in debt, and operating losses of $200M annually due to COVID. Enterprise value (assets minus bankruptcy costs) is $2.7B if reorganization costs 10% (0.10 × $3B = $0.3B). Debt holders get the first $2B, leaving $0.7B for equity. But equity holders have an option: if the airline returns to profitability, equity might be worth $1.5B (upside) or $0 (liquidation). The option value is the spread. If you believe there's a 60% chance of recovery (equity worth $1B) and 40% chance of zero, equity is worth 0.60 × $1B + 0.40 × $0 = $600M. Distressed equity holders are betting on a turnaround; it's not a valuation of current earnings, it's an option on future recovery. The stock price might be $2-5 because the equity is thin—a small move in recovery probability swings the entire value.

Leverage multiplies the recovery bet. If the airline recovers and asset value hits $3B, debt (protected at $2B) doesn't move, but equity explodes from $0.7B to $1B. That's the leverage play in distressed investing.

Where the approach breaks

Distressed-equity analysis fails when equity holders confuse probability of survival with value. Lehman Brothers' equity fell from $600 to near-zero before bankruptcy; holders convinced themselves it would survive because "Lehman has survived every crisis since 1850." Survivorship bias and sunk cost thinking caused massive losses. The failure also occurs when the analyst values distressed equity at a P/E multiple derived from normal companies. A distressed airline at $3/share trading at 0.3x book looks cheap (PE is negative, book is $8/share), but the math is that equity is nearly worthless. Terminal bankruptcy value should anchor the valuation, not mid-cycle multiples. GM's 2008-2009 distressed period showed this: equity holders held on assuming "GM will recover to 8x earnings like 2005," but recovery required bankruptcy, massive restructuring, and years of losses. The equity-as-option view says: "Equity is worth something only if the firm survives and thrives. Calculate that probability fairly, or stay out."
