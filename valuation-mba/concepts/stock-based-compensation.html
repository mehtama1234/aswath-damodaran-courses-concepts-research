Stock-based compensation

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Stock-based compensation

Meaning. Stock-based compensation is a real claim on future equity value, not a harmless non-cash expense to ignore.

Why it matters. It matters because valuation is about who owns the future cash flows, not just what operating income looks like today.

How Damodaran develops it. Loose-end sessions use SBC to show how accounting adjustments can shift value between current and future owners.

Common mistakes

  • Adding back SBC without accounting for dilution.
  • Treating employee equity as free compensation.

Worked example

A SaaS company reported 2024 operating income of $50M. SBC expense (non-cash) was $12M. Naive analysts add back SBC to get "adjusted" $62M, then DCF at 9% discount rate and 3% growth: terminal value = 62 × 1.03 / 0.06 = $1,063M. But dilution sits hidden: shares outstanding 100M, new SBC grants dilute by 8M shares annually. Over 5 years, shares become 140M. Per-share value drops from $7.60 to $8.37.

Correct approach: discount $50M operating income (SBC kept as a real cash cost), terminal value = 50 × 1.03 / 0.06 = $858M. Option-pool method: $12M SBC at $15/share = 0.8M shares/year minus 0.3M buybacks = 0.5M net annual dilution. Year 5 shares = 102.5M. Per-share value: $858M / 102.5M = $8.37.

Where the approach breaks

SBC becomes a value trap when companies use high growth to justify dilution. Between 1999-2001, tech firms granted options worth >10% of annual equity value; dilution swamped earnings growth. Second trap: assuming buybacks offset dilution perfectly. In downturns (2001, 2008, 2022), repurchases halt while option grants continue, spiking dilution from 2% to 5%. Third: SBC inconsistency across peers. Capitalizing SBC into asset cost versus expensing it makes EPS and ROIC incomparable; multiples mislead.
