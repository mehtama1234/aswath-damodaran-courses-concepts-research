Concept

Growth quality

Meaning. Growth is high quality only when it earns more than it costs, can be funded realistically, and does not destroy margins or returns.

Why it matters. The course refuses to treat growth as automatically good; growth can destroy value when reinvestment economics are weak.

How Damodaran develops it. Growth, cash-flow, and terminal-value sessions repeatedly connect growth to returns on capital and reinvestment intensity.

Common mistakes

  • Rewarding revenue growth without checking returns.
  • Assuming scale automatically improves economics.

Worked example

A fast-growing software firm forecasts 25% annual revenue growth over 5 years. To sustain that growth, it requires 60% of revenues reinvested in R&D, sales, and infrastructure. Its operating margin is 15%. That means the firm earns 0.15 × Revenue but must reinvest 0.60 × Revenue, leaving −45% of revenues as cash flow—the firm requires external funding to support this growth. The reinvestment intensity (60% of revenues) far exceeds the operating cash generation (15%), so growth is unsustainable without debt or equity issuance. For comparison, a competitor growing at 12% annually needs only 40% reinvestment and earns 15% operating margin, leaving −25% of revenues. Both require funding, but the slower-growth competitor has a less severe reinvestment gap.

The return spread matters more than growth rate. Measure this via ROIC (return on invested capital): if a firm grows 20% annually but earns only 8% returns on invested capital while the cost of capital is 9%, that firm is destroying value with each increment of growth. The growth rate multiplied by the reinvestment rate yields the sustainable internal growth: if ROIC is 8% and you reinvest 40% of earnings, internal growth is 0.08 × 0.40 = 3.2%. If the cost of capital is 9%, any value created at 8% ROIC and destroyed when discounted at 9%, since 8% < 9%. Only when ROIC exceeds the cost of capital does growth quality turn positive and expansion add value.

Where the approach breaks

Growth quality fails when a firm projects high growth without credible reinvestment economics. A telecom claiming 20% subscriber growth while maintaining 30% operating margins should trigger a flag: how is it funding the expansion? If through massive capex not yet reflected in margins, those margins will compress. If through acquisition debt, financial leverage will rise and cost of equity will climb, offsetting nominal growth benefits. The clearest historical failure: internet firms in 1999-2000 projected hypergrowth (50%+ annually) with promises of profitability 'later,' discounting at rates that assumed they'd eventually earn 15% returns. They rarely did. The boundary is crossed when the reinvestment intensity required to hit growth forecasts would leave the firm unable to fund itself at reasonable leverage levels—that is, when reinvestment intensity exceeds operating margins by such a margin that internal + prudent debt funding cannot bridge the gap, external equity issuance becomes permanent and dilutive, and growth becomes destructive rather than creative.
