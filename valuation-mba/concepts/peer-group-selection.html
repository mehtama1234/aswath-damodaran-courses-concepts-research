Concept

Peer group selection

Meaning. A peer group is an analytical choice about which firms are similar enough for relative pricing.

Why it matters. Peer choice is where pricing becomes both useful and dangerous: the answer can move with the comparable set.

How Damodaran develops it. Pricing analytics sessions use peer groups and regressions to make relative pricing more disciplined.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming peers are naturally given.
  • Picking peers because they support the target price.

Worked example

You're pricing an industrial software company with 25% EBITDA margin. "Comparable" peers range widely: Atlassian trades at 12x EV/EBITDA with 35% margins; SAP at 4x with 18% margins; Synopsys at 8x with 22% margins. If you pick Atlassian as your peer and apply 12x to your $50M EBITDA ($600M value), your company is worth $600M. If you pick SAP, 4x × $50M = $200M. Same company, $400M range. The difference is that Synopsys at 8x ($400M) trades at a multiple reflecting its actual 22% margin—closer to your profile. A disciplined peer-group choice says: "I will include only software firms with 20-30% EBITDA margins, public, $500M+ revenue, no distressed situations, same geographic exposure." That narrows to Synopsys, Altium, Anaplan-type peers. Median EV/EBITDA of 7.5-8.5x feels defensible because the comparables share your business economics.

Regression-check: Plot your peer group's margins against multiples to confirm the relationship. If your 25% margin falls between peers at 4x and 12x, interpolate to 8-9x. If it falls outside the range, your story about why you're different drives the multiple choice.

Where the approach breaks

Peer-group selection becomes manipulation when the analyst cherry-picks comparables to justify a predetermined value. Facebook's IPO pricing (2012) used peers like Zynga (declining, 5x revenue) and Groupon (near-bankruptcy, 3x revenue), inflating Facebook's justified multiple to 27x revenue. In reality, Facebook's 60%+ growth and 30%+ margins deserved maybe 15-18x revenue—still high, but not speculative. The failure also occurs when "peers" have structural differences the analyst ignores. Applying luxury-goods multiples (LVMH at 3.5x revenue) to a mass-market consumer co (30% margins, cyclical) is a category error. During 2021's SPAC mania, IPO targets were priced against peers that had recently IPO'd or merged at bubble valuations, creating a feedback loop where each new comparable inflated the next. The discipline breaks when you stop asking "why do these firms have different multiples?" and start asking "which multiple helps my deal get done?"
