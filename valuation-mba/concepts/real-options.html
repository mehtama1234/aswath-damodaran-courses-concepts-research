Concept

Real options

Meaning. A real option is the value of managerial flexibility under uncertainty when the firm owns a scarce right to wait, expand, abandon, or change course.

Why it matters. It changes the role of uncertainty: uncertainty can increase value when downside is limited and upside remains open.

How Damodaran develops it. Real options are introduced after IPOs and then applied to patents, natural resources, distressed equity, and strategic rights.

Common mistakes

  • Using option language for any uncertain growth story.
  • Ignoring the need for exclusivity and genuine flexibility.

Worked example

Merck holds a patent on a drug in Phase 2 trials. Standard DCF says: expected probability of FDA approval 10%, expected NPV if approved $2B, DCF value = 0.10 × $2B = $200M. That's not an option valuation. Real option: Merck owns the *right to wait*. Each year in trials costs $100M and gives new information. If the drug fails, Merck loses only the current year's R&D ($100M), not the full $2B. If it succeeds, Merck captures $2B. The value from waiting and learning is *not* captured in a simple discounted-probability calculation. Black-Scholes says: treat development cost as strike price ($700M cumulative if it takes 7 years), revenue potential as underlying asset (volatility ~60% in biotech), time to maturity as 7 years, rate 5%. Volatility is the key: *uncertainty makes optionality valuable*. Because Merck can abandon, the option to wait beats either (a) committing $700M now or (b) doing nothing. The option value is $500M+, not the $200M naive DCF gives. Why? Because high volatility (Phase 2 might show stunning efficacy or complete failure) creates asymmetry: downside is capped (the $100M each year), upside is open (blockbuster status).

Another case: Intel in 2015 considering a $20B fab in Arizona. Naïve DCF: chip margin 45%, fab cost $20B, 10-year payback at cost of capital 8% suggests NPV near zero—wait, don't build. Real option: semiconductor cycles mean Intel might not need the fab in 2016, but *might* desperately need it in 2019 if TSMC can't scale. The option to *expand* capacity if demand crushes supply is worth waiting for better information. Intel exercised it (building in 2018 when demand was clear), proving the wait-and-learn value.

Where the approach breaks

Real options fails when there is no exclusivity or flexibility. A utility building a power plant has no option to wait—demand is now, competitors won't wait, regulatory approval takes years. Calling it a "flexibility option" is theater; you either build or you don't. Second failure: using option language for projects that are simply *uncertain* but not *optional*. A biotech Phase 3 trial with 50% success rate isn't really an option—you're committed to spending $500M, the outcome is binary, and you can't stop mid-trial usefully. The true option examples (patents you can abandon or file suit, natural gas wells you can develop later if prices spike, real estate you can develop in phases, R&D portfolios where you kill projects) have three hallmarks: *exclusive rights* (others can't do it), *flexibility in timing* (you choose when), and *asymmetric payoff* (downside capped, upside open). If you have two out of three, you probably don't have a real option worth modeling separately from DCF.
