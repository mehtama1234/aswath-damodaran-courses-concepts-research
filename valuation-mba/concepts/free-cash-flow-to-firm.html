Free cash flow to firm

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Free cash flow to firm

Meaning. FCFF is operating cash flow available to all capital providers after taxes and reinvestment needed to sustain growth.

Why it matters. It is the cash-flow engine for firm valuation and forces the analyst to separate accounting earnings from owner-relevant cash flows.

How Damodaran develops it. The cash-flow sessions clean up operating income, taxes, reinvestment, working capital, and accounting adjustments.

Common mistakes

  • Using net income as if it were free cash flow.
  • Forgetting working capital or capital expenditures.

Worked example

Consider a manufacturing firm with EBIT of $50M, marginal tax rate 25%, depreciation $8M, capex $12M, and increase in net working capital of $2M. To compute FCFF: FCFF = EBIT × (1 - Tax Rate) + Depreciation - Capex - Change in NWC = $50M × (1 - 0.25) + $8M - $12M - $2M = $37.5M + $8M - $12M - $2M = $31.5M. This is the cash available to all investors (debt and equity holders) after the firm has reinvested to sustain operations. Many analysts mistakenly use net income of $45M (after interest deduction) and call that free cash flow, but that overstates cash to equity holders and ignores reinvestment needs. The FCFF approach cleanly separates operating economics (EBIT) from financing (interest) and reinvestment, making it firm-agnostic.

If you value the firm using FCFF = $31.5M, growth 6%, and WACC 8%, then Firm Value = $31.5M × (1.06) / (0.08 - 0.06) = $33.39M / 0.02 = $1,669.5M. Deduct net debt ($100M) and you get Equity Value = $1,569.5M. This is internally consistent because FCFF is measured before debt service, and WACC includes both equity and debt costs.

Where the approach breaks

FCFF breaks down in three scenarios. First, when working capital is volatile or inventory/receivables surge unexpectedly—FCFF can swing from positive to negative in a single year (e.g., an auto supplier that builds inventory for a new model). Analysts who forecast stable FCFF growth miss the cash crunch and overvalue the firm. Second, during distress: if capex is deferred and working capital is released (accounts payable delayed), FCFF looks strong today but the firm is starving itself. Third, in acquisitions with integration costs: if a buyer assumes the target's current FCFF but doesn't account for one-time capex to merge systems, FCFF projections will be too high. Real options and strategic value, which FCFF cannot capture, often dominate acquisition returns.
