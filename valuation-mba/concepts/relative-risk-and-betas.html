Concept

Relative risk and betas

Meaning. Beta is a measure of relative risk, but useful beta estimation has to reflect business mix and leverage rather than blindly trust regressions.

Why it matters. The course uses beta to discipline risk measurement while warning against false precision from noisy market data.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran moves from regression betas to bottom-up thinking and alternatives when firms are private, changing, or thinly traded.

Common mistakes

  • Taking a regression beta as truth.
  • Forgetting that financial leverage changes equity beta.

Worked example

A conglomerate operates two businesses: retail (business beta 1.1) with 40% of assets, and telecommunications (business beta 0.7) with 60% of assets. The unlevered beta is 0.4 * 1.1 + 0.6 * 0.7 = 0.86. The firm has 30% debt (market value) at a pre-tax cost of 5% and 70% equity, with tax rate 25%. Unlevered cost of equity = 4% + 0.86 * 6.5% = 9.59%. Relever for leverage: beta_equity = 0.86 * (1 + (1 - 0.25) * 0.30 / 0.70) = 1.136. Cost of equity = 4% + 1.136 * 6.5% = 11.38%. This is discipline: the beta is built from business economics, not a regression on past stock returns, and the leverage adjustment is mechanical and transparent.

Where the approach breaks

This fails when betas are taken from Bloomberg regression output and used unchanged. A real example: a company restructures from 10% debt to 40% debt, but the analyst keeps using the old regression beta of 1.2, ignoring that the equity beta will climb to around 1.7. The valuation is contaminated. Another boundary: private companies and small-caps where regression betas are noise—the method works only when you build bottom-up betas from comparable businesses in the same industry. The approach also breaks when business mix is changing—a tech company acquiring telecom assets needs a new unlevered beta, not a historical average. Real failure: when analysts forgot to relever betas when a company went from 5% to 50% debt during leverage buyout financing.
