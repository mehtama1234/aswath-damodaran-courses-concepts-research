Concept

Control value

Meaning. Control value is the incremental value from changing who makes decisions and what decisions get made.

Why it matters. A control premium is justified only if new control can improve operations, financing, payout, investment, or strategy.

How Damodaran develops it. The final session closes the course by returning valuation to governance, decision rights, and the power to change outcomes.

Common mistakes

  • Paying a control premium for voting rights alone.
  • Assuming every acquirer can run the target better.

Worked example

A public company trades at $40 per share (market cap $1B; 25M shares outstanding). A buyer acquires a 20% stake in the open market at $40, then launches a tender offer at $52 for the remaining 80%. The control premium is 30% of market price. How much of the total $1,240M acquisition cost is control value?

First, separate value per share before vs. after the control shift. The acquirer forecasts $80M annual FCFF that the current management wastes on poor capital allocation, but which a new owner can redeploy. Valuing $80M FCFF at 9% WACC and 2.5% terminal growth yields 80M × 1.025 / (0.09 - 0.025) = 82M / 0.065 = $1,262M for the whole firm (rounded). The public value was $1B; the control value adds $262M.

The total price paid is: 5M shares (20%) at $40 = $200M, plus 20M shares (80%) at $52 = $1,040M, for $1,240M total. Breaking this down: $200M to acquire minority stake, $1,040M to acquire control. Of the $1,240M paid for the entire firm, $262M is justified control value (new management's improvement in capital allocation), and the remaining $978M ($1,240M - $262M) is the residual, which the deal must justify through synergies, overpayment, or residual market-price consensus.

Example two: a majority owner of a private business values it at $500M but a minority stake holder demands a 30% discount ('minority discount') for lack of control, yielding $350M. If the business is stable and earns $50M FCFF, the disagreement reflects whether minority shares receive governance rights or are frozen out of decisions. The control-value difference is real here, not just a negotiating number.

Where the approach breaks

Control-value analysis fails when analysts assume all control shifts are beneficial. Overpaying for control—or assigning value to a restructuring plan that never materializes—is a standard failure. The 2001 AOL-Time Warner merger paid $162B assuming the buyer would unlock synergies and impose tighter capital discipline; the acquirer destroyed most of that value because integration proved chaotic and the synergies were illusory. The boundary is also crossed when control discounts and premiums are applied asymmetrically. If a majority holder discounts minority stakes by 25% to 35%, but uses the full equity value for debt covenants or earnings metrics, the accounting is inconsistent. Control premium/discount is not a fixed number; it depends entirely on what the controlling owner can credibly achieve differently—which is often far less than a buyer hopes during negotiation.
