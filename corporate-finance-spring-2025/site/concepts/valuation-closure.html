Valuation closure

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Valuation closure

Meaning. The integration point where objective function, risk, investment quality, financing mix, and payout policy are converted into value.

Why it matters. Valuation is the final audit: every corporate finance choice should eventually show up in expected cash flows, growth, risk, or terminal value.

How Damodaran develops it. The closing sessions make valuation the course's synthesis rather than a separate topic bolted on at the end.

Common mistakes

  • Treating valuation as only a modeling exercise.
  • Ignoring how earlier corporate finance choices feed cash flows, risk, and growth.

Worked example

Johnson & Johnson: synthesize every corporate-finance choice into valuation. Objective function: maximize shareholder value (not revenue, not market share). This drives dividend payout of 45-50% of earnings (returns cash, signals confidence, tax-efficient). Year 1 FCF (after payout choice): $20B. Growth assumption: 6% for 5 years (new drug launches drive this; without payout discipline, R&D pipeline would be underfunded, growth would be 2%). Terminal growth: 2% (mature pharma, GDP-bound). Debt-equity tradeoff: J&J runs at 20% debt-to-total-capital (low leverage because of patent cliffs and cyclicality; this keeps cost of debt at 3.5% and cost of equity at 8%). Cost of capital = 0.80 × 8% + 0.20 × 3.5% × (1 - 0.21) = 6.953% ≈ 7.0%. Valuation = Year 1 FCF × PV(growing 5-year cash flows) + Terminal value / (1.07)^5. That's $20B × $4.77 annuity factor + $546B / (1.07)^5 ≈ $95B + $389B = $485B enterprise value. Less net debt $15B = equity value $470B. Per share ÷ 2.5B shares ≈ $188. Each choice (payout, debt level, growth investment, leverage) shows up *here*, in the final number. If J&J cut R&D to boost near-term profits, growth drops to 3%, and the valuation falls even if payout rises, because terminal value is built on sustainable growth and competitive position.

Contrast: a private equity firm's buyout of a company uses *higher* debt (50% ratio), assumes aggressive capex cuts and margin expansion, lower terminal growth (2% not 4%), and expects to exit at 8× EBITDA multiple in 5 years. Same company, different objective function (cash-on-cash return, not perpetual growth) and financing (leverage). Valuation = $500M to a PE buyer versus $700M to a strategic buyer, because the PE plan harvests cash faster and values growth less.

Where the approach breaks

Valuation closure fails when earlier corporate-finance choices are ignored or misaligned. Lehman Brothers in 2006 used 40% leverage (debt-to-assets), justified by low short-term funding costs (4%) and high-yield assumptions on mortgage-backed securities (8% returns). Valuation built in that capital structure. When mortgage spreads blew out (costs rose to 8%+, yields fell to 4%), the DCF broke because (1) cost of capital jumped (leverage was now dangerous, not optimal), (2) FCF turned negative (mortgage origination was unprofitable), (3) terminal value collapsed. The valuation that looked solid at $60B in 2007 evaporated; the company was worthless by September 2008. The lesson: capital structure, payout policy, and growth investments are not *independent* inputs—they're an ecosystem. If you lever aggressively to finance growth, but growth evaporates, the leverage *destroys* value. If you cut payout to invest, but investments return less than cost of capital (ROIC < WACC), you're destroying value in real-time. A sound valuation catches this: it shows how each choice feeds into FCF, growth, and risk. If the math doesn't work (e.g., borrowing at 5%, investing at 3% ROIC), no amount of spreadsheet beauty fixes it.
