Concept

APV and peer group financing approaches

Meaning. Alternative ways to assess financing mix: APV values tax benefits and distress costs directly, while peer analysis uses comparable-company behavior as evidence.

Why it matters. They check the cost-of-capital approach from different angles and reveal when market practice or model assumptions deserve skepticism.

How Damodaran develops it. The course uses APV and peer groups after WACC optimization to show that financing decisions can be triangulated rather than dictated by one model.

Common mistakes

  • Using peers as proof instead of evidence.
  • Forgetting that APV still requires judgments about debt benefits and costs.

Worked example

Take a firm with an unlevered enterprise value of $1,200M, derived from operating cash flows of $80M growing at 2.5% perpetually, discounted at an 8% unlevered cost of capital. The firm has no debt today but is considering borrowing $500M at a 5% cost. With a 25% tax rate, the annual tax shield on interest is $500M × 5% × 25% = $6.25M. Assuming that debt remains perpetual, the present value of these tax shields is $6.25M / 8% = $78.125M.

Now subtract bankruptcy costs. If the firm estimates that financial distress would cost 3% of firm value to navigate (lost sales, management time, legal fees), that's $1,200M × 3% = $36M. The net tax benefit is $78.125M - $36M = $42.125M. APV values the levered firm at $1,200M + $42.125M = $1,242.125M. If the firm stays unleveraged or uses less debt, the tax shield shrinks but bankruptcy risk disappears, pushing value closer to the unlevered base.

The optimal debt choice requires testing the tradeoff across multiple debt levels, tracking how tax rates, interest coverage, and default probabilities change as debt grows. A second tranche of $300M borrowed would face higher rates (6% instead of 5%) and carry higher default risk, so each additional dollar of debt creates less incremental tax benefit and more distress cost, revealing where the margin of benefit turns negative.

Where the approach breaks

APV fails when the analyst treats the tax shield and discount rate inconsistently. If the unlevered cost of capital already embeds expectations of tax benefits (because comparable firms are already leveraged and the beta reflects that fact), adding the tax shield as a separate PV component double-counts the benefit. Alternatively, if the firm's debt cost already incorporates expected default losses, then separately subtracting a bankruptcy cost estimate double-counts the penalty. The method also breaks down in unstable or cyclical industries where debt levels fluctuate and leverage-dependent default risk cannot be treated as static. Finally, APV can produce nonsensical answers if cash flows themselves depend on leverage (e.g., when higher debt changes operational strategy so dramatically that the unlevered cash-flow estimate is no longer a defensible baseline).
