Cost of equity

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Cost of equity

Meaning. The return equity investors require for bearing the risk of the firm's residual cash flows.

Why it matters. It is the equity hurdle rate for projects and the equity discount rate in valuation.

How Damodaran develops it. The course builds cost of equity from riskfree rates, premiums, and betas, turning risk into an explicit required return.

Common mistakes

  • Treating cost of equity as what the company wants to earn.
  • Using one corporate cost of equity for projects with different risk.

Worked example

A stable utility earns 8% returns on capital and faces a riskfree rate of 3%, equity risk premium of 5.5%, and beta of 0.65. Its cost of equity is 3% + 0.65 × 5.5% = 6.575%, lower than its expected ROIC because utilities are low-risk cash generators. If the same firm adds renewable-energy development (riskier, higher-volatility business), its beta rises to 0.85, pushing cost of equity to 3% + 0.85 × 5.5% = 7.675%—a 110 bps increase. This illustrates why sector mix matters: a diversified utility is cheaper to finance than a merchant renewable developer despite both earning similar accounting returns.

Second example: cost of equity changes with leverage. A firm with unlevered beta 0.80 (business risk only) financed entirely with equity has an equity cost of 3% + 0.80 × 5.5% = 7.4%. If it adds 40% debt at 5% pre-tax cost (with 25% tax rate = 3.75% after-tax), the firm's levered beta jumps to 0.80 × (1 + (1 - 0.25) × 0.40 / 0.60) = 1.20, pushing equity cost to 3% + 1.20 × 5.5% = 9.6%. The firm's WACC falls to 0.60 × 9.6% + 0.40 × 3.75% = 7.26%—lower than the all-equity case because the tax shield on debt offsets the higher financial risk. But equity holders now face 9.6% cost of equity, not 7.4%, a 220 bps jump from their perspective.

Where the approach breaks

Cost of equity goes wrong when analysts treat it as a static corporate number rather than a claim-specific return. A firm cannot have one cost of equity for all its divisions; a conglomerate with a 10% corporate cost of equity that assigns it uniformly to high-risk venture units and low-risk utilities will overvalue the utilities and underfund growth units. Beta calculated from market regressions becomes unreliable for firms that change their business mix (like Apple shifting from a hardware/PC company to services) or for private firms with thin trading and lumpy deal activity. The boundary failure is sharp with leveraged buyouts: paying 5× EBITDA for a firm at 3× existing leverage, then adding leverage to 6×, transforms equity cost from 9% to 20%+ almost immediately. Analysts who valued the firm pre-LBO at 9% cost of equity and then used the same rate post-LBO are pricing phantom value—they've ignored that the same cash flows now go to more-senior claimants (debt), leaving equity holders bearing magnified risk.
