Concept

Objective function

Meaning. The operating target for corporate finance: make decisions that increase the value of the business, while being explicit about whose claim is being maximized.

Why it matters. Without an objective function, investment, financing, and payout decisions become political preferences with numbers attached.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran opens the course by forcing the end-game question before any spreadsheet mechanics: managers need a target before they can judge tradeoffs.

Common mistakes

  • Treating value maximization as a slogan instead of a discipline.
  • Changing the objective when the answer becomes uncomfortable.

Worked example

A company with $1B market cap of equity faces an investment decision: acquire a competitor for $300M. Standalone, the target earns $20M after-tax (20% ROIC) with 3% growth. Synergies (cost cuts): $8M annually forever. Two frameworks: First, 'maximize total stakeholder value'—employees, customers, suppliers, community. The acquisition creates $15M yearly synergies but eliminates 2,000 jobs (local community cost). Without monetizing community value, the decision hangs. Second, 'maximize equity value': The acquisition adds NPV = ($20M + $8M) / (8% - 2%) - $300M = $280M. But debt holders take 30% of synergies as lower credit risk (cost of debt falls 0.5%, worth $50M to bondholders). Equity gets $230M. If the objective is equity value, the deal is worth it. The objective function determines the answer. If the objective is 'maximize all stakeholder claims,' the job losses must be offset or the deal is rejected. Clarity on whose claim is being optimized is non-negotiable.

Where the approach breaks

The objective function fails catastrophically when undefined or when it shifts mid-project. Real case: Enron's management optimized for stock price in the short term, which required consistent earnings growth at any cost. The 'hidden' objective—hidden even from themselves—was personal compensation (stock options). This misalignment destroyed value. When the objective shifted (discovered fraud led to criminal liability), management had already optimized the company into bankruptcy. Another failure: companies claiming to maximize 'total value creation' while actually maximizing manager compensation. Decisions look defensible until they are seen to have enriched management at shareholder expense—then the objective function is revealed as a lie. Also breaks: when the objective function is 'maximize revenue' instead of 'maximize value.' A $10B revenue company earning 2% net margin creates less value than a $2B revenue company earning 15% net margin. Objectives matter because they discipline tradeoffs. Without clarity, each stakeholder will assume the objective is whatever maximizes their own position.
