Concept

Project returns

Meaning. Measures used to judge whether projects clear their hurdle rates and create value.

Why it matters. Return measures are useful only when they are cash-flow based, time-consistent, and compared to the right risk-adjusted hurdle rate.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran uses the project-return sessions to separate investment quality from accounting appearance.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing accounting returns to market hurdle rates.
  • Using IRR mechanically when scale or timing distorts the answer.

Worked example

An oil company evaluates a $500M project with cash flows: Year 0: -$500M. Year 1-5: $150M annually. Year 6-20: $80M annually. Perpetual value (year 21+): $60M / (8% - 2%) = $1,000M. Discount rate 8% (project risk). NPV at year 0: PV(Years 1-5) = $150M × 3.99 (5-year annuity factor at 8%) = $598.5M. PV(Years 6-20) = $80M × 8.56 (15-year annuity factor at 8%) × 1/(1.08)^5 = $684.8M × 0.6806 = $466M. PV(Terminal perpetuity at Year 20) = $1,000M / (1.08)^20 = $215M. Total NPV = $598.5M + $466M + $215M - $500M = $1,280M (rounded). IRR is approximately 16%. Both NPV and IRR are positive; the project clears its 8% hurdle with substantial margin. Time-weighted returns: The $500M is invested upfront but cash flows are realized over 20+ years; the return is not constant but accelerates as the perpetual component contributes significant value. The company recovers its investment in years 3-4 and earns excess returns in years 5-20. This temporal shape matters for capital budgeting—it affects risk (long-tail uncertainty in perpetual cash flows) and financing (does the firm have staying power for a 20-year commitment?).

Where the approach breaks

Project returns fail when computed on accounting rather than cash flows. A common error: using accounting return on invested capital (net income / capital) instead of IRR or NPV. A $100M manufacturing facility with $10M annual net income shows 10% accounting return. But if the factory requires $3M annual capex to sustain operations, the true cash return is ($10M - $3M) / $100M = 7%. The accounting number hides reinvestment needs. Another boundary: scale and timing distortion. Two projects, A and B, both with 15% IRR. Project A invests $1M and returns $1.5M in year 1 (15% IRR, recovers immediately). Project B invests $1B and returns $1.15B in year 10 (also 15% IRR, but capital is locked up 10 years). Same IRR, wildly different value and risk. NPV ranking would correctly favor A. IRR ranking alone is silent on scale. Real case: When a firm compares a high-IRR, small project to a lower-IRR, larger project, IRR ranking can mislead—NPV is the tiebreaker. Also breaks: when projects are mutually exclusive and ranking differs between NPV and IRR due to discount-rate assumptions, the analyst must be explicit about which metric drives decision. Many firms mechanically use 'IRR > hurdle rate,' missing that NPV is the proper criterion for value creation.
