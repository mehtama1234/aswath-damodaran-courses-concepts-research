Equity risk premiums

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Equity risk premiums

Meaning. The extra return investors demand for holding equities instead of a riskfree asset.

Why it matters. The ERP is one of the largest moving parts in cost of equity, and small changes can swing investment and valuation conclusions.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran develops the premium as a market price of risk rather than a fixed historical constant, with country risk and forward-looking estimates entering the discussion.

Common mistakes

  • Blindly using long historical averages.
  • Ignoring country risk or current market pricing.

Worked example

The risk-free rate is 2.5% (US 10-year Treasury yield). An investor can earn 2.5% with zero risk by holding Treasury bonds. To invest in equities, she requires extra return to compensate for volatility and the possibility that the company fails. Historically, equities have returned about 7–8% annually above Treasuries over the long term. This is the equity risk premium (ERP). Using 5.5% as a reasonable current estimate: cost of equity = risk-free rate + ERP = 2.5% + 5.5% = 8%. A company with stable, predictable cash flows might have a beta of 0.8 (less volatile than the market), so its cost of equity is 2.5% + 0.8 × 5.5% = 2.5% + 4.4% = 6.9%. A high-growth technology company with beta of 1.5 has a cost of equity of 2.5% + 1.5 × 5.5% = 2.5% + 8.25% = 10.75%. The ERP difference drives a massive wedge: the tech company must earn 3.85 percentage points higher returns than the utility to justify its risk. If both earn 8%, the utility is overvalued (earning more than required) and the tech company is undervalued (earning less than required). In valuation, ERP is one of the largest moving parts. A 0.5 percentage-point change in ERP (from 5.5% to 6.0%) increases the cost of equity for the tech stock from 10.75% to 11.25%, which reduces its DCF value by roughly 5–8% depending on growth rates.

Where the approach breaks

Equity risk premium assumptions fail when historical averages become disconnected from forward expectations. During the dot-com bubble (1995–2000), investors accepted ERP of 2% or less, believing tech stocks were low-risk because they were growing fast; valuations soared to 100x PE, implying the ERP had shrunk to nearly zero. When the bubble burst and tech crashed 80%, the realized ERP was negative for years, and investors demanded much higher premiums (ERP spiked to 8–9%). Similarly, after the 2008 financial crisis, stock returns lagged bonds for a decade (negative realized ERP) because investors were terrified of equities and demanded outsized risk premiums. The forward-looking ERP (what investors require going forward) is not directly observable; it must be inferred from market prices, surveys, or historical averages, each of which can mislead. When ERP estimates vary from 4% to 7% depending on the source, the 3 percentage-point range is enormous—it is the difference between a stock being expensive and cheap. The most treacherous failure is using a single historical ERP in all economic regimes: if you use 5.5% to value stocks in 2008 (when investors were demanding 7–8%), you overpay by 30–40%. Conversely, using 7% in 2021 (when investors were accepting 4–5%) understates returns. The threshold for caution is when ERP differs by more than 1.5 percentage points across credible sources; at that point, sensitivity analysis is mandatory.
