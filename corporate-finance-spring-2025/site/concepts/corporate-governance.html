Corporate governance

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Corporate governance

Meaning. The system that determines whether managers act for owners, themselves, or other power centers around the firm.

Why it matters. Governance is the enforcement mechanism behind the objective function; weak governance lets value-destroying choices persist.

How Damodaran develops it. The early governance sessions turn abstract value maximization into a control problem: who has power, how is it checked, and what happens when nobody is watching?

Common mistakes

  • Assuming boards automatically protect shareholders.
  • Ignoring how dispersed ownership weakens discipline.

Worked example

Company A is a mid-cap industrial firm with widely dispersed shareholders (largest owner holds 2%, no blockholder). The board is 8 directors, 7 independent, 1 CEO. Compensation: CEO gets $2M salary + $8M annual bonus tied to EBITDA targets (hit 85% of bonus every year), no long-term equity vesting. Dividend policy: 80% payout ratio, never cut since 2003. Capital allocation: $500M in revenue, spends $100M annually on R&D (20%), but no major innovation launched in 8 years. The numbers say: management maximizes short-term earnings (bonuses hit 85% every year—a gift that says targets are too easy), avoids long-term risk (R&D spend is political protection, not investment strategy), and hides behind dividends (shareholders see 80% payout as stability; executives see it as cash removal that prevents hostile takeovers). Now contrast Company B: same industry, similar size, but blockholder owns 35% (founder/family). CEO compensation is $1.5M base + long-term equity vesting over 5 years, no short-term bonus. Dividend policy: 30% payout, reinvest earnings into adjacent markets. Capital allocation: $100M R&D annually (20%), launched 3 new products in 5 years. The gap is governance. Company A's dispersed ownership and weak board allow management to optimize for themselves (easy bonus targets, political payout policy). Company B's blockholder oversight forces alignment: the founder's equity stake means she wins when long-term value grows, so R&D and retained earnings benefit her directly. Arithmetic: Company A has destroyed value (no innovation in 8 years means the $500M revenue stays flat, margins erode as costs rise). Company B has grown revenue at 12% annually because new products opened new segments. By Year 10, Company A is $500M revenue at declining margins. Company B is $1.4B revenue. Governance is the mechanism that explains why one business's capital discipline and the other's capital waste.

Where the approach breaks

Governance fails when oversight is ceremonial, when the CEO and board chair are the same person (or close allies), or when no shareholder can dislodge bad management. First failure: Enron had a board, auditors, and governance rules. But the CEO and CFO colluded, the board deferred to management, and dispersed shareholders had no power to intervene before the collapse. Governance systems require an actual willingness to challenge, not just a checkbox board. Second failure: Wells Fargo maintained a board and governance structure that appeared sound but allowed middle management to commit fraud (fake accounts) while executives denied knowledge and collected bonuses. The governance system did not prevent value destruction because no one at the top was incentivized to look. Third failure: Viacom and Paramount under Sumner Redstone (founder, majority owner, elderly, minimally active). The company made disastrous strategic bets (Blockbuster, declining TV), executives got rich, shareholders got poor, but dispersed minority shareholders could not remove the board because Redstone owned >50% and was unaccountable. Fourth failure: Facebook/Meta's single-class share structure (Mark Zuckerberg retains 60% voting control despite owning 14% economic stake) meant that governance oversight is non-binding. Even when the board disapproved of major decisions, Zuckerberg could overrule. The boundary is crossed when: (1) the CEO or founder controls both the company and the board, (2) majority shareholders are present but inactive, (3) compensation rewards short-term metrics at the expense of reinvestment, or (4) the payout policy is too high relative to reinvestment needs (hiding a weak business).
