Cost of capital optimization

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Cost of capital optimization

Meaning. Estimating the debt ratio that minimizes the weighted average cost of capital and maximizes firm value.

Why it matters. It gives the financing decision an explicit value test instead of relying on peer imitation or managerial instinct.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran walks through changing debt ratios, ratings, debt costs, equity costs, and firm value to locate a financing mix.

Common mistakes

  • Optimizing WACC without updating default risk.
  • Treating the current debt ratio as automatically optimal.

Worked example

A manufacturing firm has an unlevered (all-equity) cost of equity of 8%. Its tax rate is 25%. Today, the firm has no debt. The cost of debt (interest rate on borrowing) is 4%. Using the capital asset pricing model, the levered cost of equity rises as debt increases because equity becomes riskier: at 20% debt-to-capital, levered cost of equity = 8% + (8% - 4%) × (0.20 / 0.80) × (1 - 0.25) = 8% + 0.75% = 8.75%. The cost of debt remains 4%, but the firm saves 25% in taxes on interest paid. The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) = (0.80 × 8.75%) + (0.20 × 4% × (1 - 0.25)) = 7% + 0.6% = 7.6%. At 40% debt, the levered cost of equity rises to 10%, and WACC = (0.60 × 10%) + (0.40 × 4% × 0.75) = 6% + 1.2% = 7.2%. At 60% debt, the levered cost of equity balloons to 12.5%, WACC = (0.40 × 12.5%) + (0.60 × 4% × 0.75) = 5% + 1.8% = 6.8%. The minimum WACC occurs around 40% debt, where the benefit of the tax shield on debt is balanced against the rising cost of equity from financial risk. This is the optimal capital structure: it minimizes the hurdle rate and maximizes firm value. A firm at 20% debt is underleveraged (it could borrow more and reduce WACC), while one at 80% debt is overleveraged (creditors demand high rates, cost of equity explodes, and WACC rises).

Where the approach breaks

Cost-of-capital optimization fails when the assumed debt capacity is illusory or when leverage increases bankruptcy risk non-linearly. A company optimizing WACC at 50% debt looks valid on paper, but if industry downturns cause revenue to halve (as in 2008 for construction companies or 2020 for airlines), the debt service becomes unsustainable and the firm enters distress. The cost of debt assumption (4% in the example) is only valid if leverage remains moderate; at 60% debt, credit rating downgrades might spike the cost of debt from 4% to 8%, which instantly erases the tax benefit and makes the high-leverage strategy catastrophic. The failure threshold is when a firm approaches or exceeds the debt level at which its credit rating falls by two notches (e.g., from A to BBB or BBB to junk). At junk-bond status (below BBB), debt costs spike 3–5 percentage points, and refinancing becomes unreliable. Financial-engineering mistakes often optimize WACC during benign conditions (low rates, stable growth) and then explode when conditions shift: Altria optimized leverage during the 1980s–2000s when tobacco cash flows were stable; when public-health pressure reduced smoking, fixed debt obligations became onerous. The optimization model also ignores agency costs (creditors imposing covenants, limiting strategic flexibility) and refinancing risk. If optimal debt needs to be rolled over during a credit crisis, the firm may not be able to refinance at assumed rates. The safest approach is to target a debt level one or two notches more conservative than the mathematical optimum, sacrificing 10–20 basis points in WACC for resilience.
