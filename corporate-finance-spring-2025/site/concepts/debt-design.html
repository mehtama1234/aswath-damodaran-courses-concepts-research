Concept

Debt design

Meaning. Choosing the currency, maturity, rate structure, and covenants of debt so financing matches the firm's asset cash flows.

Why it matters. Even the right amount of debt can be dangerous if it is the wrong kind of debt.

How Damodaran develops it. After optimizing debt levels, Damodaran turns to debt design: match financing to how the business earns cash.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at the debt ratio.
  • Borrowing in a currency or maturity that does not match operating cash flows.

Worked example

A firm with operating cash flows of 100M USD annually decides between three debt designs: (1) 10-year fixed-rate bond at 5% in dollars; (2) 10-year floating-rate bond at LIBOR + 200 bps; (3) 10-year fixed-rate bond at 6% in euros. The firm earns revenues primarily in dollars. Option 1 matches cash flow and debt currency, locking in borrowing cost. Option 2 creates refinancing risk: if LIBOR rises to 4% (from 1%), the firm's cost of debt jumps to 6%, stretching cash flows. Option 3 creates currency mismatch: if the dollar strengthens 15% versus the euro over 10 years, the euro-denominated debt becomes 15% more expensive in dollar terms, a hidden cost of $8-10M annually. For this firm, option 1 is clearly superior: duration matches (10-year assets need 10-year financing), currency matches, and rate certainty allows planning. The cost of currency/rate mismatch can be 50-100 bps on total cost of capital if misaligned.

Second example: a mining firm with commodity revenues (volatile in any currency) might rationally choose floating-rate debt because margins are already volatile; locking in a fixed 5% borrowing rate creates a fixed cost that amplifies downside risk in low-commodity-price scenarios. Instead, floating rates let debt cost decline alongside revenue, preserving financial flexibility. This is not a mistake; it is a design choice aligned with business structure.

Where the approach breaks

Debt design fails when firms borrow in expensive or mismatched structures and rationalize them with vague risk arguments. A UK firm earning sterling operating cash flows but borrowing 10-year fixed at 4% in yen (because yen rates were artificially low in the 2010s) was gambling on yen depreciation, not managing financial risk. When the yen strengthened unexpectedly in 2012-2014, the firm faced massive hedging costs or balance-sheet pain. Similarly, extending maturity beyond the economic life of the underlying assets creates refinancing risk: a 30-year bond for a business with 15-year cash-flow visibility is mismatched. The firm may be forced to refinance when rates are high or credit quality has deteriorated, turning a chosen capital structure into an accident waiting to happen. The clearest boundary: a firm that issues long-term debt in a foreign currency it does not earn, without hedging, is betting on currency markets, not managing debt structure—a core failure of design discipline.
