Growth and reinvestment — growth only adds value when it earns more than it costs

Companies grow. Investors cheer. But growth is not free — it requires money plowed back into the business. Whether that growth enriches the owners depends entirely on whether the money earned on that reinvestment beats the cost of that capital. Here is why the same 15% growth can be worth $14 a share or $43 a share depending on the return it earns, why most "growth stocks" deserve skepticism, and where capital allocation lives inside the valuation machine.

1 · The problem — growth doesn't automatically mean richer owners

A company can grow revenue 15% a year for a decade and still make its shareholders poorer. How? Because to achieve that growth, it must spend money — hiring, building factories, buying inventory, funding R&D. That spending is called reinvestment. If the money earned on that reinvestment falls short of what it cost to raise the capital, the company has used shareholders' cash to fund growth that destroys value.

Listen to analysts and the question vanishes: ‘Growth is good. We are excited about growth.’ No one asks whether the growth is worth what it costs. Yet that question settles everything.

2 · The reframe — growth requires reinvestment, and reinvestment has a cost

Two facts reframe this. First, growth is not generated by wishful thinking. To grow revenue, you must invest cash. This is reinvestment — money the business keeps, not cash paid to shareholders. The amount required is proportional to the growth rate: faster growth demands more plowed-back cash.

Second, that capital has a cost of capital — the return that investors could demand for their money, reflecting how risky it is. For a typical company, this is 8–10% per year. If the business invests money and earns less than that return on it, the capital would have been better deployed elsewhere. Earnings that exactly match the cost of capital create zero value; earnings above it create value; earnings below it destroy it.

Put them together: value is created not by growth itself, but by the spread — the return on reinvested money minus the cost of that capital.

3 · Seen on a real run — the same 15% growth, three different returns

We ran a valuation engine on an example company, Northwind, with 15% revenue growth and the same story in all three cases except one number: the return earned on the money plowed back in. That return is called the return on invested capital, or ROIC. Three versions show what happens when ROIC changes but growth stays the same.

return on reinvested capital (ROIC)enterprise value per share
6% per year (below the 9% cost of capital)$14.41
9% per year (exactly equal to cost of capital)$22.60
20% per year (well above cost of capital)$43.23
The discovered fact: identical growth produces wildly different value based on the return that growth earns. At ROIC 6%, shareholders would be wealthier if the company did not grow at all. At ROIC 9%, growth adds nothing, just treads water. At ROIC 20%, it creates wealth.

4 · Why the spread is what matters — the return versus the cost

Look at the three columns and the pattern is unmissable: value follows the spread, not the growth. In the first case, the company earns 6% on every reinvested dollar but must pay 9% to raise that capital. It loses 3 cents on the dollar, steadily destroying value even while growing. In the second case, earning exactly 9% on capital that costs 9% yields nothing — the growth is worth zero; shareholders would be indifferent between growth and distributing that cash. In the third case, earning 20% on capital that costs 9% is a win; the spread of 11 cents per dollar compounds and creates wealth.

This is the hidden lever in capital allocation. When a company promises growth, the only question that matters is: how much value is the spread generating? A "5x revenue" unicorn with ROIC below the cost of capital is not a treasure — it is a destruction machine that looks good until the cash runs out. Conversely, a mature, boring company that grows 3% a year at ROIC 25% may be creating far more value.

The reason this lever is hidden is that management rarely admits it, and accountants never highlight it. Revenue growth is visible and celebrated. The ROIC earned on that growth is harder to measure and rarely discussed. Yet it is the real story.

5 · The one real weakness — ROIC on future investment is hard to forecast

The idea is airtight in theory. In practice, the spread hinges on a forecast: what will the return be on money invested tomorrow? For a mature company with a proven business, you can estimate ROIC by looking at history. For a startup or a company entering a new market, the forward ROIC is a guess. And it is a guess executives are incentivized to make optimistic.

Worse, accounting returns can mislead. A company may report a high operating margin that looks like ROIC, but if growth requires surprising amounts of reinvestment (hidden in working capital or long-term assets), the true ROIC is lower than it appears. You can reach toward this by studying how much capital the company ties up to produce each dollar of profit, but the data often hides in the footnotes.

The humbling truth: the valuation machine is only as good as the ROIC forecast you feed it. Assume too high a ROIC and you'll overpay. Assume too low and you'll miss good opportunities. The discipline is in asking the question precisely; the humility is in admitting the answer is uncertain.

6 · The surprise — this reframes what "moat" and "quality" actually mean

Once you see that value lives in the spread, the whole language of investing shifts. A "moat" — a competitive advantage that protects market share — is really a durable source of ROIC above the cost of capital. A "quality" company is not one that has high margins or fast growth; it is one that reinvests at high returns. And most importantly, value-destroying growth is common enough that it redefines what makes an investment safe.

This concept is baked into every other tool in the course. When you estimate a terminal value for a company, you are implicitly forecasting a long-run ROIC and asking whether it beats the cost of capital. When you build a margin of safety, you are protecting yourself against the risk that the ROIC you assumed is too high. When you reverse-engineer a stock price to ask what growth it implies, you are asking whether the market is assuming an ROIC that makes sense.

Learn to see every valuation through the lens of the spread — growth rate times the gap between ROIC and cost of capital — and you begin to spot the companies where growth enriches you and the ones where it merely burnishes the narrative.
