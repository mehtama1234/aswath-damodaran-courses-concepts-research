Side costs and side benefits

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Side costs and side benefits

Meaning. The indirect effects a project imposes on the rest of the business, including cannibalization, synergies, brand effects, and strategic spillovers.

Why it matters. They are where project analysis stops being isolated spreadsheet work and becomes firm-level capital allocation.

How Damodaran develops it. The course explicitly asks students to bring side consequences into project choice instead of pretending each investment stands alone.

Common mistakes

  • Treating synergy as a free add-on.
  • Ignoring cannibalization because it is politically inconvenient.

Worked example

A retail chain with 2,500 stores operates a web platform (accounting for 5% of sales, $50M annually). The CFO proposes a new omnichannel project: spend $20M upfront, sync store and online inventory, and launch same-day delivery from stores. NPV calculation of the omnichannel project alone: incremental online sales $80M over 5 years, costs $30M (delivery logistics, systems), incremental profit $50M. Discount at 12%, NPV ≈ $18M. Project looks good, approve it. But wait: if customers can now order online and pick up in stores, some fraction (say 20%) of current in-store traffic diverts to online. That cannibalization: 2,500 stores × 5M visitors/year × 20% × $50 average transaction × 20% margin = ~$25B store margin gets hit per year. Over 5 years, you lose $125B in gross margin. The NPV is now $18M - $125B = -$124.98B. Cancel the project. Or, pivot: instead of same-day delivery, use the omnichannel platform to increase average store visit frequency (e.g., 'order online, get 20% off in-store pickup'). Now cannibalization is -10% instead of -20%, so net margin loss is $62.5B, and incremental online profit is $50M. NPV = $50M - $62.5B ≈ -$62.45B. Still negative. Or pivot again: launch same-day delivery but only for categories that have low in-store attachment (pet supplies, light bulbs). Cannibalization drops to 5%, margin loss is $31.25B, and NPV = $50M - $31.25B = -$31.2B. Now the project creates value only if incremental customer lifetime value or competitive moat effects are worth >$31.25B. The arithmetic forces you to name the side costs explicitly and test whether benefits overcome them.

Where the approach breaks

Side-cost and side-benefit analysis breaks when synergies are announced without numbers, when cannibalization is politically forbidden to mention, or when the boundaries are drawn to exclude inconvenient externalities. First failure: a bank merges with a broker (say JPMorgan + Bear Stearns in 2008). The stated synergy: $3B cost saves (duplicate back-office, branch consolidation). The omitted side cost: Bear Stearns had $50B in mortgage-backed securities that are now JPMorgan's problem. When those assets deteriorate, the 'synergy' becomes a catastrophe. The boundary: JPMorgan acquired Bear to prevent systemic risk, but the NPV framework would have shown negative value due to hidden liabilities. Second failure: a tobacco company launches a nicotine-replacement product (gum, patches) and claims it is a new-growth initiative. The stated benefit: $100M in new revenue, $25M profit. The omitted side cost: it cannibalizes 30% of cigarette volume (which had 65% margins, so $200M margin loss). Net value: -$175M. But if the company does not acknowledge that cannibalization, the project gets approved as 'diversification.' Third failure: Kodak's digital camera division was profitable on a standalone basis, but it cannibalized film (Kodak's core 80% margin business). Kodak's capital allocation system was blind to the side cost, so it starved digital investment. Other companies without film legacy (Sony, Canon) invested heavily in digital and captured the market. The boundary is crossed when: (1) synergies are claimed without being quantified, (2) cannibalization is dismissed as 'negligible' without a base-case calculation, (3) the project scope is drawn to exclude related businesses that could be affected, or (4) side benefits (brand lift, platform optionality, customer stickiness) are vague and never stress-tested.
