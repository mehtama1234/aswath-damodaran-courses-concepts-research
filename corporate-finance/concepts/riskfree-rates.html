Riskfree rates

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Riskfree rates

Meaning. The base rate for discounting cash flows in a currency, stripped of default risk and matched to the currency of the cash flows.

Why it matters. Every hurdle rate starts here; a wrong riskfree rate contaminates valuation, project analysis, and capital structure choices.

How Damodaran develops it. The risk block begins by separating currency, inflation, default risk, and maturity so the discount rate has a defensible anchor.

Common mistakes

  • Using the government bond yield mechanically even when default risk exists.
  • Mixing a dollar riskfree rate with non-dollar cash flows.

Worked example

In 2024, suppose you are valuing a US company with cash flows in dollars. The 10-year US Treasury yield is 4.0%, embedded inflation is ~2.3%, so the real riskfree rate is approximately 4.0% - 2.3% = 1.7%. If you forecast a company's FCFF in nominal dollars (including inflation), you must discount at the nominal riskfree rate of 4.0%. (If you forecast real FCFF, you discount at 1.7%—consistency is essential.) A company forecasts FCFF of $50M next year, growing 4% nominally for 10 years, then 2% forever. FCFF in year 11 is $50M × (1.04)^10 × 1.02 = $75.5M. Using WACC = 4% (riskfree) + (1.2 × 5.5% equity risk premium) + (0.4 × 3% after-tax cost of debt - weighted) = 4% + 6.6% + 1.2% = 11.8%, Firm Value = PV(years 1-10 FCFF) + PV(Terminal Value at year 11). Terminal Value at year 11 = $75.5M / (0.118 - 0.02) = $770.4M; discounted to today at 11.8% for 11 years ≈ $246M. This riskfree rate of 4% is the bedrock; if it were wrong (say, you used 3%), WACC would be 10.8%, and the Terminal Value would be $75.5M / (0.108 - 0.02) = $856M, with PV ≈ $281M (14% higher). Conversely, if inflation-adjusted rates in another currency were 3% (e.g., euro-denominated cash flows), the riskfree rate must be the 3% euro rate, not the 4% dollar rate.

Where the approach breaks

Riskfree rates break when default risk is embedded in the government bond yield. In 2011, Greek 10-year bonds yielded 15%+, but the riskfree rate in euros was the German 10-year bond at ~2%. Using Greek yields as a riskfree rate for a Greek company would yield an absurdly high WACC and undervalue the firm. In emerging markets, sovereign spreads are often 2–5%, creating confusion: a 6% Brazilian government bond yield includes default risk, not the true riskfree rate (which is closer to the real US Treasury at 1.7% + Brazilian inflation expectations of 3–4% = 4.7% in nominal terms, adjusted for currency risk). In periods of extreme monetary policy—when central banks hold rates below inflation (negative real rates)—using the nominal government yield can be misleading. From 2012–2021, real yields were negative in the US and Europe, yet companies still had positive required returns. Using nominal yields mechanically would have set riskfree rates too low. A third failure: if you are valuing a non-dollar company but use a dollar riskfree rate, you have a currency mismatch. If you forecast cash flows in Indian rupees and use a 4% dollar riskfree rate, you will underestimate the discount rate (the rupee riskfree rate is higher due to inflation and currency risk premiums).
