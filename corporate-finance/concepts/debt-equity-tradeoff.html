Debt-equity tradeoff

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Debt-equity tradeoff

Meaning. The balance between the benefits of debt, especially tax shields and discipline, and its costs, especially distress, agency conflicts, and lost flexibility.

Why it matters. Capital structure is not about liking debt or equity; it is about finding where financing choices increase firm value.

How Damodaran develops it. The financing block begins with the tradeoff and then tests it through cost-of-capital, APV, peer-group, and design lenses.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming more debt is always cheaper.
  • Ignoring business risk and flexibility needs.

Worked example

A manufacturing firm with EBIT = $100M and tax rate 30% is considering capital structure. Scenario A (all equity): 10M shares at $50 = $500M market value. WACC = 10% (cost of equity, no tax shield). Firm value = $100M × (1 - 0.30) / 0.10 = $700M. Equity value = $700M, per share = $70. Scenario B (30% debt at 5% cost): Debt = $210M, Equity = $490M. Tax shield from debt = $210M × 0.30 × 5% = $3.15M in annual tax savings (PV ≈ $63M assuming perpetual debt). Cost of equity rises to 12% due to financial risk. WACC = (490/700) × 12% + (210/700) × 5% × (1 - 0.30) = 70% × 12% + 30% × 3.5% = 8.4% + 1.05% = 9.45%. Firm value = $100M × (1 - 0.30) / 0.0945 = $740M. This is $40M higher than all-equity due to tax shields (approximately $63M in discounted shields less some additional financial cost). Equity value = $740M - $210M = $530M; per share = $53 (despite higher leverage, per-share equity value is lower because of increased risk). The tradeoff is clear: tax shields boost firm value, but excess leverage increases bankruptcy risk and reduces equity value per share.

Scenario C (60% debt at 6% due to higher risk): Debt = $420M, Equity = $280M, cost of equity = 16% (distress looming). WACC = (280/700) × 16% + (420/700) × 6% × 0.70 = 40% × 16% + 60% × 4.2% = 6.4% + 2.52% = 8.92%. Firm value = $100M × 0.70 / 0.0892 = $784M (now $84M higher than all-equity). Equity value = $364M; per share = $36.40 (equity value has fallen despite higher firm value because debt holders capture the upside and shareholders face larger distress costs). Optimal structure is around 30–40% debt in this case.

Where the approach breaks

The debt-equity tradeoff breaks in several ways. First, in distressed or volatile industries: if a firm's EBIT can swing from $100M to -$20M in a downturn, adding debt at 5% cost locks in a fixed claim that may be impossible to pay. In 2008, many financial institutions were 30x leveraged; when asset values fell 10%, equity was wiped out. The tax shield disappeared as the firm failed. An airline with volatile revenue should use less debt than a toll-road operator with stable cash flows, yet industry norms are often applied uniformly. Second, the tradeoff ignores flexibility: a firm that is 40% debt-financed has little cushion to invest in new projects or weather downturns. If a new opportunity requires $100M and equity financing is expensive, the firm cannot pursue it. A firm with excess debt capacity can borrow at lower rates and move faster. Debt-equity optimization that maximizes firm value may minimize strategic optionality. Third, when agency costs are high: a manager with free cash flow and no debt oversight will spend on pet projects. Debt disciplines the manager. But too much debt and the manager is incentivized to take excessive risk ("heads I win as equity holder, tails the bondholders lose"). A private equity sponsor adding debt post-acquisition can boost returns short-term but leave the company fragile if operations slip. The tradeoff model often assumes symmetric information and rational actors; reality has asymmetric information, stakeholder conflicts, and behavioral biases that push capital structures away from theoretical optima.
