Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Incremental cash flows

Meaning. The cash flows that change because the project is taken, including working capital, taxes, side effects, and opportunity costs.

Why it matters. Project analysis lives or dies on incrementality; accounting categories and sunk costs can make bad projects look good or good projects look bad.

How Damodaran develops it. The investment section shifts attention from reported earnings to actual cash-flow consequences of a decision.

Common mistakes

  • Counting sunk costs.
  • Ignoring opportunity costs, working capital, or cannibalization.

Worked example

Kraft Heinz is considering a $500M automated factory to replace 4 aging manual plants. What's the incremental cash flow? Start with gross profit gain: new factory will produce 20% more output per worker, so on $2B annual capacity, you save 18 months of current operating costs = $800M (labor, utilities, inefficiency). After-tax: $800M × (1 - 0.25 tax rate) = $600M benefit year 1. But incremental cash flow requires accounting for *all* changes. (1) Sunk cost: the $50M in consultant fees to design the factory. Already spent. Not incremental. (2) Opportunity cost: the 4 old plants can be sold for $200M salvage value. The new factory eliminates that option. Include $200M as an opportunity cost (foregone proceeds), after-tax = $150M. (3) Working capital: a larger factory needs more inventory and receivables. Initial working capital outlay = $30M. Recoverable at end. (4) Side effect (cannibalization): the new efficiency might cannibalize 10% of sales from another Kraft brand (say, competing spread products). That's $100M lost revenue at 40% margin = $40M pre-tax loss. After-tax: $40M × (1 - 0.25) = $30M after-tax loss per year. True incremental FCF year 1 = $600M savings - $150M opportunity cost - $30M working capital - $30M cannibalization = $390M. Compare to the $500M capex at hurdle rate 10%, and NPV = $390M / 1.10 - $500M ≈ negative in year 1, but over 10 years with growth, it likely clears.

Key inputs that change the answer: (1) How much does cannibalization actually occur? If it's 5% not 10%, the lost margin is $20M pre-tax, or $15M after-tax, adding $15M to incremental cash. (2) Can you avoid the working capital outlay by running the old plants leaner first? If not, $30M is real.

Where the approach breaks

Incremental-cash-flow analysis fails catastrophically when companies ignore sunk costs, double-count opportunity costs, or miss cannibalization. Example: Kodak's decision in 1998 to open a digital imaging facility. Accounting showed $300M annual profit potential (new revenue stream). But incrementally: (1) It would cannibalize 25% of film profits ($150M annually). (2) Building required $800M capex, and there was no buyer for the old film plants ($200M opportunity cost). (3) Working capital for digital inventory was $100M. True incremental cash flow = $300M - $150M - $200M opportunity - $100M working capital = -$150M the first year. Yet the project went forward because accountants counted gross profit, not incremental cash. The company lost money for 5 years. Second failure: treating all side effects as optional. A pharma company develops a new drug that works for Disease A ($2B market potential). But it also shows efficacy in Disease B. If the Disease B indicator gets approved, it cannibalizes Disease A prescription (same patients, smaller Disease B population). Many projects assume both markets independent. The truth: incremental cash flow must include the *realistic mix* of outcomes, not best-case.
