Concept

Cost of debt

Meaning. The current market cost of borrowing, driven by default risk, tax effects, maturity, and currency.

Why it matters. Debt is not cheap just because coupon rates are low; the relevant cost is the current pre-tax and after-tax cost of borrowing.

How Damodaran develops it. Damodaran connects ratings, default spreads, interest coverage, and taxes to make debt cost a live financing input.

Common mistakes

  • Using book interest expense divided by book debt.
  • Ignoring default risk or tax shields.

Worked example

A BBB-rated industrial firm has $200M of debt. Book interest expense is $10M (5% coupon). But the relevant cost of debt for valuation is the market's current cost of borrowing, not history. Today's BBB-rated 10-year spread is 2.0% over UST (riskfree rate 4.2%), so market yield = 4.2% + 2.0% = 6.2% pre-tax. Tax rate = 25%. After-tax cost of debt = 6.2% × (1 - 0.25) = 4.65%. This is the input for WACC, not the 5% coupon. If interest coverage ratio is low (EBIT $50M / Interest $10M = 5x), the firm is close to junk territory—next refinancing might cost 7% or more.

WACC Example: Cost of equity 10%, Cost of debt (after-tax) 4.65%, Debt = $200M, Equity market value = $600M, Total capital = $800M. WACC = (600/800 × 10%) + (200/800 × 4.65%) = 7.5% + 1.16% = 8.66%. If you had used the book 5% coupon instead: WACC = (600/800 × 10%) + (200/800 × 3.75%) = 7.5% + 0.94% = 8.44%. A 22 bps error in WACC compounds over valuation.

Where the approach breaks

Cost of debt breaks when using book interest expense as current cost. A company that issued 10-year bonds at 3% five years ago now faces 6.5% rates at refinancing—the old 3% coupon is irrelevant for valuation of future cash flows. The bond may still trade at par (3% coupon + 3.5% yield on new risk), but the marginal cost of new borrowing is 6.5%. Similarly, cost of debt fails if you apply the same rate to every project; a firm with BBB rating overall might borrow $50M for a risky expansion at 7% or $100M for a safe debt paydown at 5.5%. The boundary is when credit spreads widen suddenly (crisis event) or firm leverage rises above historical norms, pushing the firm toward downgrade. A firm at 40% debt-to-value with stable coverage can use its current 6.2% cost; a firm at 60% debt-to-value heading toward 70% should forecast higher marginal cost of debt for future borrowing.
