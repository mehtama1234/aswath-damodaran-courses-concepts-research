Bottom-up betas

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Bottom-up betas

Meaning. A beta estimated from the business or businesses a firm operates in, adjusted for operating and financial leverage.

Why it matters. Bottom-up betas are Damodaran's answer to noisy regressions, private companies, changing business mixes, and thin trading.

How Damodaran develops it. The beta sessions move from market regressions toward business-risk estimation, using comparable firms and leverage adjustments.

Common mistakes

  • Trusting a single regression beta without asking what business it measures.
  • Forgetting to unlever and relever betas when financial leverage changes.

Worked example

A diversified manufacturer operates a packaged-food division (unlevered beta 0.8), an industrial machinery division (unlevered beta 1.2), and a distribution arm (unlevered beta 0.9). Revenue split: 40% / 40% / 20%. Bottom-up unlevered beta for the firm = 0.40 × 0.8 + 0.40 × 1.2 + 0.20 × 0.9 = 0.98. The firm has $2B debt, $3B equity (D/E = 0.67). Tax rate 25%. Relevering: β_levered = 0.98 × [1 + (1 - 0.25) × 0.67] = 0.98 × 1.5 = 1.47. Cost of equity = 4% + 1.47 × 5% = 11.35%. This approach captures business mix and leverage without relying on a single regression beta that might be noisy from thin trading in any one division.

Where the approach breaks

Bottom-up betas fail when business risks are not independent or when leveraging formulas assume a stable capital structure. First failure: conglomerate discount. A firm with divisions in oil, tech, and retail gets a combined beta of 1.0, but the market prices it at a discount because management cannot allocate capital across unrelated industries efficiently. The true equity risk (and thus required return) is higher than the bottom-up beta suggests. Second failure: leverage assumption. Damodaran's relever formula assumes perpetual debt and constant tax rates. If a firm is deleveraging or faces rising interest rates that change tax-benefit timing, the formula breaks. Example: a utilities firm with 70% debt at 2% rates (cost of debt 4%) is stable; if rates jump to 6% and refinancing forces occur, new leverage becomes more risky than the old formula predicted. Third: business-risk estimation error. Classifying a fintech payment processor as 'fintech' (high beta) vs. 'payments processor' (lower beta) changes the result by 0.3+ beta points. Small categorization mistakes compound through leverage adjustments.
