Dividends and buybacks

Overview Thesis Themes Subthemes Concepts Evidence Discussions Sessions

Concept

Dividends and buybacks

Meaning. The two main ways firms return cash to shareholders, each carrying different tax, signaling, flexibility, and trust implications.

Why it matters. Payout policy is where excess cash, reinvestment discipline, and shareholder trust become visible.

How Damodaran develops it. The payout block starts with dividends and buybacks as tools, then asks when returning cash is the best use of corporate resources.

Common mistakes

  • Treating dividends as automatically good.
  • Treating buybacks as automatically manipulative or automatically value creating.

Worked example

A mature industrial firm generates $500M free cash flow annually. It needs $150M for maintenance capex and growth projects, leaving $350M in discretionary cash. Tax rate on dividends = 20%, on buybacks (capital gains) = 15% (lower holding periods assumed). Dividend route: pay $350M dividend, 1000M shares outstanding, $0.35 per share dividend. Tax cost to shareholders = $350M × 20% = $70M. Buyback route: repurchase $350M at stock price $50/share = 7M shares retired. Shares drop to 993M. EPS growth from shrinkage: if net income stays $350M, EPS rises from $0.35 to $350M / 993M = ~$0.3525. Tax cost to sellers = ~$52.5M (15% on $350M gain realized by selling shareholders). Buyback saves $17.5M in taxes, and non-selling shareholders defer gains indefinitely.

Same $350M cash leaving the firm; different tax efficiency and signals. Dividend is sticky (cut it = bad signal), buyback is flexible (stop it = no surprise).

Where the approach breaks

Dividends and buybacks fail when applied as default policy instead of following cash and reinvestment. A high-growth tech firm with $100M free cash flow but $200M reinvestment needs should not distribute any cash; both dividend and buyback destroy value. Conversely, a firm generating $500M FCF but reinvesting only $100M is wasting capital if it keeps $300M in cash earning 2% while cost of capital is 10%—returning cash is mandatory, not discretionary. Buybacks break when they are funded with debt increase: 'we bought back $1B in stock while debt rose $1B' is just leverage, not shareholder return. Historical case: S&P 500 firms buying shares at peaks (2007, 2021) and selling at troughs destroyed value—buyback discipline failed. Another boundary: if buyback at $50/share looks cheap but insider selling is heavy, the market price may reflect better-informed insiders, and the buyback destroys value even if the stock later falls. Dividends fail as a return policy if the payout is so high that the firm must cut growth spending or raise debt—the dividend becomes a sign of poor capital allocation, not shareholder-friendly policy.
